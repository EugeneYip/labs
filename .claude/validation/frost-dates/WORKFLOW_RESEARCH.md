## The job

1. **Plan ahead.** Count seed-starting and planting-out dates from the last spring frost.
   - The North American seed-packet convention gives sowing as "weeks before or after last frost" [S].
   - Johnny's calculator expects you to type in your own date [V].
   - Planners look the date up and count for you (Seedtime, GrowVeg) [V].
2. **Choose a level of risk.**
   - US guides separate a 50% "last frost date" from a later, roughly 10% "frost-free date" [V].
   - UK tools aim for dates that hold six years in seven (GardenFocused) or carry a 10% risk (My Cozy Plant) [V].
   - Frost Dates' median plus its 9-in-10 date fits this need [repo].
3. **Decide night by night.** The forecast decides when to cover plants. Soil temperature decides whether seeds will grow [V/S].
   - Experts treat the frost date as a baseline beside those two. Purdue's specialist puts it as history for planning, current conditions for daily decisions [V].
4. **Autumn.** First-frost dates serve autumn crops and season extension. Midwest growers use thresholds below 28 °F for high tunnels [V].

## Pain

1. **The station isn't where the garden is.**
   - A ZIP spanning sea level to 2,500 ft gave a spring date nearly a month early [V].
   - Stations half a mile apart differed [V].
   - UK tools came out a month early for one Hertfordshire grower [S].
2. **Sources conflict, often by a month or more.**
   - London: Airtasker says mid-March, while Garden Ninja's zone runs from late April to mid-May [V/S].
   - Canberra: mid-September to early November [V/S].
   - Christchurch: mid-August to late October [V].
   - Causes include different definitions (average, 30%, 10%, latest ever), air against ground frost, city stations against gardens, and dates guessed from zones [V].
   - A new tool adds one more number to that list.
3. **The odds are confusing.** Popular guides use "90%" in opposite senses [S]. Tools sit at 30%, 50% or about 14% [V].
4. **False springs cost plants.** A Michigan tomato loss, UK May frosts on beans and potatoes [V/S]. The usual answer is fleece and the forecast [V].

## Microclimates

**Expert advice:**
- A hard frost can come at least 10 days either side of the average [V].
- Valleys collect cold air (Met Office, DWD) [V].
- Add 1–3 weeks for frost pockets (NZ nurseries) [V].
- Add a week and watch your own site [S].

**What gardeners do:**
- Ask an experienced neighbour.
- Find a closer station.
- Add two weeks.
- Protect plants [V/S].

**Ground frost.** It's more common than air frost in May (Met Office) [V], and the archive measures air at 2 m.

## What the accuracy check adds (`notes/accuracy-check.md`)

**Frost-prone inland valleys.** At Canberra, Frost Dates' cautious date is about 5 weeks earlier than two station records, and it shows half the stations' frost nights [V, own check].
- A gardener there gets a worse answer than the folk rule.
- The page's general warning about hollows and valleys points the right way, but doesn't convey a month-long error across a city.

**Maritime climates.** The frost-anchored calendar advises tomatoes outside 1.5–2.5 months before local guidance: Dublin Mar 12–19, London Apr 11–18, Christchurch Sep 14–21 [V, own check].

**Continental climates.** Dates and calendar are reasonable: Minneapolis tomatoes go out May 14–21 counted from the cautious date [V, own check]. This matches the earlier NOAA check of US cities [repo].
