# Workflow research: how custody schedules are chosen, agreed, and lived

Comparative Research (2026-10-11). Internal; not legal advice. Full evidence: `notes/market-notes.md` §1–2 and `notes/legal-risk-notes.md` §1, §3. Labels: **[V]** read on the source; **[S]** snippet; **[U]** inference. Levels: **(O)** official (courts, statutes, guidelines), **(P)** professional or vendor practice, **(A)** individual anecdotes and reviews.

## Where schedules come from

- **Court model plans and defaults (O)** [V]:
  - Arizona's *Planning for Parenting Time* sets out 14 plans by child age, each with 1–2-week sample grids (plan 13 is "a 5-2-2-5 schedule"), and says it isn't legal advice.
  - Indiana's Parenting Time Guidelines point to an official online calendar on the court portal that builds a 12-month color-coded calendar with holidays, birthdays, and school calendars (announced January 2018; the interactive tool wasn't inspected).
  - Texas's Standard Possession Order (1st, 3rd, 5th weekends, alternating holidays, summer block) has free calendar generators made by law firms [V].
  - California's holiday attachment (FL-341(C)) is a table with "every year", "even-numbered years", and "odd-numbered years" columns, and says holidays take priority over regular time.
- **Self-help and legal-aid builders** (Oregon Guide & File, Illinois Legal Aid Online, Maryland's form) produce court forms, not visual calendars, as far as seen [S].
- **Professionals (P)**: mediators moved from flip charts to software years ago (Custody X Change, 2014 account) [V]; OurFamilyWizard's Custody Schedule Builder (2026) is pitched for showing co-parents schedules in mediation and court [V].
- **Templates and content (P)**: OurFamilyWizard's free 2-2-5-5 spreadsheet; law-firm pattern explainers; an alcohol-monitoring company's 2-2-5-5 article with sample clauses as a lead magnet [V].

**Gap:** official guidance stops at templates and tables; turning them into real dates is left to parents, apps, or (in Indiana) a state tool [V].

## Patterns

2-2-3, 2-2-5-5 (also called 5-2-2-5), 3-4-4-3, alternating weeks, every other weekend with or without a midweek visit, and statutory defaults like Texas's SPO. Equal-time plans are flagged in Arizona's guide as suiting only parents who can agree [V].

## Holidays

Holidays override the regular rotation (Arizona, California) and alternate by year parity, with Mother's and Father's Day often fixed [V]. Platforms automate this as a paid feature (Kidtime premium) or leave it manual; general calendars need one-off events [V/S]. Every source points to holiday rotation as the hard part [V]. Custody Calendar swaps single nights by hand and has no even/odd-year rules.

## Overnights: why they matter and why they're hard

Many child-support guidelines adjust support by parenting time, often keyed to annual overnights with thresholds (O) [V]:

| Place | Rule (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| Ohio | 90 or more court-ordered overnights a year cuts support 10% |
| Pennsylvania | 40% or more of overnights creates a presumption of reduction |
| Indiana | Credit from 52 overnights; an overnight must include care, not just a bed; equal time is 181–183 |
| Florida | "Substantial" time-sharing is at least 20% of overnights |
| Virginia | 24-hour days, with a half-day rule for short overnights; formula applies above 90 days |
| New Jersey | An overnight is more than 12 hours of a 24-hour day, not where the child sleeps |
| Arizona | Days built from blocks of hours; a Thursday 9 p.m. to Monday school drop-off stay is 3.5 days (the page counts 4 nights) |
| Minnesota, Oregon | Averaged over two years |
| UK (Child Maintenance Service) | Night bands at 52, 104, 156, 175 |
| Australia | Care bands at 52, 128, 176, 190, 238, 314 nights |

So the page's count ("each night goes to whoever has the children that night") is a planning count, not a legal time-share; it's least safe near a threshold, which is exactly when people care [V/U]. Minnesota's court self-help centers publish a handout on counting overnights, so staff evidently field the question [S].

## How schedules are shared

- **Orders describe schedules in words and tables** (O) [V]. Some courts name apps: an Oregon form lists eight (OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents, Custody X Change, WeParent, 2Houses, AppClose, CoParenter, Fayr); a Colorado checklist names two; a Missouri form makes OurFamilyWizard the exclusive channel, calendar included, renewed until the youngest child turns 18 [V].
- **Professionals exchange PDFs and printouts** (P) [V].
- **Parents use platform calendars or general calendars** (A/P): a county family-services handout lists apps alongside Google Calendar; a 2018 ABA article called OurFamilyWizard and Google Calendar the two most-cited programs; parents leaving paid apps revert to Google or iPhone calendars, texts, and invites [V].

## Pain

- **Platform calendars** are called hard to navigate, buggy, and poor at non-traditional schedules; one reviewer said clunkiness bred suspicion between co-parents (A) [V].
- **Cost and lock-in**: "court-ordered" recurs in reviews; annual-only billing, both parents paying, no refunds (A) [V].
- **AppClose's 2026 paywall** displaced a free user base; reviewers called it a bait and switch because courts had ordered it for being free (A) [V].
- **Sync**: the most specific OurFamilyWizard requests are automatic push and two-way integration with personal calendars (A) [V].
- **Percentages**: some value totals for court (Alimentor reviewers); a Kidtime reviewer alleged wrong percentages; a mediator warns percentages shift attention from quality to quantity (A/P) [V].

## After agreement, the job changes

The recurring job is shared, live state between two households (who has them on date X, changes, swaps) plus records of what happened. That is what people pay for and what courts order. A static snapshot link and a one-time calendar file don't do it, and the page says later changes don't reach a shared link [V/U].

## By segment

| Segment | Job frequency | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Parents comparing before agreement or modification | Episodic; large population (about 1 in 4 US children under 21 lived with one parent with the other outside the home in 2022) [V] | Least falsified; privacy and no account matter before cooperation; no platform ordered yet |
| Post-agreement planners | Recurring | Weak: already in Google Calendar or a platform |
| Mediators | Several families a week [U] | Plausible but contested: OurFamilyWizard's builder is free for them |
| Parenting coordinators | High-conflict cases | Poor: records matter more |
| Attorneys and paralegals | Moderate | Poor: bundled tools |
| Court self-help and legal aid | Frequent questions | A channel, not a user base; slow and accuracy-sensitive |
