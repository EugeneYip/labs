# Workflow research: how orientation to day and time is supported today

Comparative Research (2026-10-11). Internal. Full evidence: `notes/market-notes.md` §1–2 and §5, `notes/device-risk-notes.md` §1–3. Labels: **[V]** read on the source; **[S]** snippet; **[U]** inference. Levels: **(a)** official care guidance, **(b)** common practice, **(c)** individual caregiver accounts. Nothing here is medical advice.

## The job, kept in three parts

1. **Orientation aid** (what guidance supports): helping someone see the weekday, date, and time of day without asking. Day Clock's core.
2. **Reminder display**: on-screen prompts at set times. Guidance supports reminders generally, with caveats that they fit some people and confuse others. Day Clock's reminders are silent and visual.
3. **Medical or safety device**: monitoring, medication management, alarms that must be heeded, help buttons. **Day Clock is not this and must never be presented as this.** Sudden new confusion about day and night can signal illness needing a doctor; a clock is never a substitute for clinical attention (a) [V].

## What official guidance says (a)

- **NHS** (reviewed January 2025): clocks showing the day and date as well as the time are useful daily-living aids; tablets and phones with reminders help many people [V].
- **Alzheimer's Society Factsheet 437** (last reviewed April 2019, possibly superseded): automatic calendar clocks help; some show morning or evening, useful when it's light in the evening or dark in the morning; clock apps exist for tablets and phones; if the person already uses a tablet, try adapting it with an app; introduce technology early [V].
- **Alzheimer's Society shop guidance**: choose for the individual, involve them, ask an occupational therapist before buying; a memory aid's presence can remind someone of their memory problems and upset them; a reminder device may help one person and confuse another [V].
- **Alzheimer's Association**: for repeated questions, answer calmly and use memory aids such as clocks and calendars, as long as they still mean something to the person; go along with a person's sense of time when they're living in an earlier period [V].
- **Dementia Australia** (2025–26): clocks with time, day, and date; day/night clocks; check every clock is correct, because a wrong time adds to confusion [V].
- **Evidence base:** the formal research concerns structured reality-orientation and cognitive-stimulation therapy in groups, not a clock left in a room (Cochrane reviews named only; scholarly, not resolved via LibKey) [U]. No controlled study of home day clocks was reviewed.

## Today's toolkit (b)

Dedicated plug-in day clocks (7–15 inches; weekday, date, time, often part of day and dimming); paper calendars, tear-off pages, and whiteboards updated each morning; talking clocks; smart speakers and displays; tablets with an app, often an old iPad or a cheap Fire or Android tablet left plugged in; in care homes, orientation boards, bedside clocks, digital signage, and above all staff prompts [V].

## Pain (b, c)

- **Repeated questions and night confusion are real** [V]: up at 6 a.m. for a 1 p.m. pickup; 3 a.m. calls about appointments. AM/PM labels stopped working for one mother, and a clock showing "morning" or "night" helped; Factsheet 437 singles out morning/evening displays [V]. This is the pain Day Clock's part-of-day words address.
- **But results are mixed** (forum threads 2015–2026) [V]: clocks helped early for some; others were hidden, ignored, looked at but not understood, or prompted "how does it work?"; telling someone to look at the clock made one man search the room and become more confused. Several caregivers said the clock helped the family stay oriented more than the person.
- **Reminders cut both ways** [V]: some reviewers report fewer repeated calls; others report anxiety when told about events too early, reminders that don't get someone out the door, and alarms switched off.
- **Remote updating is the most consistent request** [V]: carers living hundreds of miles or a country away want to change messages and reminders from a phone, and prefer a dedicated device that won't be left uncharged.
- **Setup and upkeep fall on the caregiver** [V]: for hardware, confusing switches, power-cut resets, manual daylight-saving changes ("dementia clock not working" and "how do you change the time" appear in autocomplete); for tablets, stopping the screen from sleeping, keeping the person from closing the app, forgotten passcodes, unplugging, and battery drain.

## Is "reuse an old tablet" a genuine advantage?

**Real, but narrow and contested.**
- Caregivers do it: an old iPad on a wall running Calendar Clock; Fashmel on an old Hudl tablet; RecallCue on a cheap Fire kept plugged in; ShowTime on an iPad no longer receiving updates, stable for over a year with Guided Access (c) [V]. Factsheet 437 endorses adapting existing devices (a) [V].
- But it's not unique: RecallCue and Memory Care Clock already market exactly this [V].
- And Day Clock's version is weaker than it sounds: it needs Safari 16.4, so iPads stuck on iPadOS 15 (iPad Air 2, iPad mini 4, and older) are outside its target, while the free same-name iPad app runs on iPadOS 15 [V]. On Android it needs Chrome 111 or later, which means Android 7 or newer; Fire tablets from 2017 and earlier run Fire OS 5, and their browser versions aren't published [V].
- Reported problems with tablets as clocks: screen sleep, locking to one app, passcode lockouts, unplugging, and anecdotal reports of swollen batteries in tablets kept on charge permanently (no official source links 24/7 charging to swelling; anecdotal, unknown frequency) [V/S].

## How often

The person looks at it daily; the caregiver's decision (which clock, how to set it up) is occasional. "Which dementia clock?" threads recur every year or two on caregiver forums [V].

## Who

- US: about 7.4 million people aged 65+ with Alzheimer's in 2026, and nearly 13 million unpaid dementia caregivers [V]. About 11% of caregivers lived an hour or more away (AARP, 2020) [S].
- Caregivers living with or near the person can set up and maintain a local tablet. Those living away need remote control, which Day Clock can't offer.
- Occupational therapists and dementia advisers route families to products through catalogues and trusted listings (Living Made Easy, Alzheimer Scotland's technology service) [V].
