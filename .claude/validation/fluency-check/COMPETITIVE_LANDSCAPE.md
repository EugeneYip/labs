# Competitive landscape: Fluency Check

Winner Validation Phase 1 (research 2026-10-10 and 11). Internal. Full evidence and sources: `notes/competitor-notes.md` (map, prices, wedge verdicts, brand check) and `notes/distribution-notes.md` (search results, free web tools, pricing). Labels: **[V]** read on the source; **[S]** snippet or secondary; **[U]** unverified or inference. Items marked **[V, rechecked]** were also confirmed directly on 2026-10-10 or 11 during synthesis.

## Verdict

- **No single feature of Fluency Check is unique.** Tap-to-mark scoring, any passage, free use, no account, and on-device storage each exist elsewhere.
- **What survives is a combination:** no account, nothing leaves the device, no audio, any passage, live scoring by the teacher, a printable marked record, any device, and offline use. No other tool found has all of these.
- **That combination matters most to a narrow group:** individuals who must or prefer to work outside school systems and cloud tools (independent tutors, some interventionists, private-school and privacy-cautious teachers) doing informal checks. Whether they value it enough to switch from paper is unproven.
- **Where it doesn't compete:** official screening and progress monitoring in US public schools (mandated platforms), schools on Wonders (built-in tool), and schools where teachers accept Microsoft's or Google's free AI tools.

## The map

### 1. The incumbent default: timer, paper, and free passages

Stopwatch, examiner copy with running word counts, slash marks, quick arithmetic, and a graph by hand or spreadsheet. Free passages: DIBELS 8 and Acadience (licensed to schools and districts only), UFLI Fluency Checks (free for non-commercial use with attribution), Newsela's printable fluency sheets with running counts [V]. Teachers describe it as quick, and the best-selling monitoring products on Teachers Pay Teachers are paper passage sets [V]. **This is the real competitor.**

### 3. Built into a core curriculum

- **McGraw Hill's Oral Reading Fluency Application** (Wonders, Maravillas, and related programs): pick a student and a passage with a goal, 60-second timer, tap each misread word, tap the last word read, get words per minute and errors against the goal, attempt history, a line graph, class averages, and a list of students below goal [V, rechecked 2026-10-11]. It uses the program's passages. **For a Wonders school, this is Fluency Check's core interaction, already inside the curriculum.**

### 6. Free web calculators and trackers

About 35 free WCPM calculators, timers, running-record calculators, and trackers rank in the top 10 for the queries checked, many dated 2026 [V]. Examples checked directly:
- **wcpmcalculator.com**: live timer, total errors typed in (no word marking), session history on the device, progress dashboard, no registration, free [V, rechecked].
- **readingcoachkit.com**: enter WCPM scores by date and see them against Hasbrouck–Tindal percentiles with a trend line; data stays on the device; free; sponsored by Heggerty and Amplify; accounts announced; a school email is required for PDF downloads [V, rechecked].
- Others (lans.cloud, ReadLivo, Elite Home Tutoring's timer, Literacy Arcade): timers with error counts typed in afterwards [V].

None checked marks specific words on the passage during the reading, keeps a marked record, and needs no account, all at once.

### 7. Spreadsheets and Teachers Pay Teachers

- About 19 Google Sheets or Excel WCPM trackers ($2–$13) mostly have 0–2 ratings; a $2 whole-class tracker has 19 [V].
- Paper passage sets sell: the $20 "60 Fluency Passages for Progress Monitoring" has 4,150 ratings [V].
- Free tools: NCII's Excel progress-monitoring tool, the Meadows Center's graph template, Intervention Central's probe generator (snippet only) [V/S].

## Wedge hypotheses, tested

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Works with any passage | **Fails as a unique wedge** | Reading Progress, ReadingFluency.app, Reader Meter, and F&P's universal module also take any text. True only against district platforms, where it's also a weakness: no norms or equivalent forms, and DIBELS passages can't legally be retyped into it |
| No account | **Holds as a fact; value unproven** | Every full-featured alternative needs an account or a school tenant; only bare calculators and small apps don't. Teachers already have Google or Microsoft accounts, so the friction saved may be small |
| No student data leaves the device | **Holds; timely, but not shown to drive choice** | No other full-featured tool keeps everything local. The 2026 voice-recording backlash is real, but districts moved to approved alternatives, not standalone tools, and a 2025 research preprint found teachers rarely choose apps for privacy |
| Faster than paper | **Weak** | It removes counting, arithmetic, and graphing, but every digital tool does, and pasting a passage plus printing a student copy can eat the gain. Unmeasured |
| Simpler than district platforms | **Holds, narrowly** | One page versus licences, rosters, and training, but teachers don't choose between them, and ReadingFluency.app and McGraw Hill's tool are also simple |
| Usable before a school adopts anything | **Weak** | True, but so are paper, easyCBM Lite, ReadingFluency.app's free tier, and spreadsheets |
| Useful for tutors and interventionists outside district systems | **Holds conditionally: the best candidate** | They can't buy district platforms or use DIBELS materials under its licence, and Microsoft's and Google's tools need a school tenant. But they need a legal passage source (UFLI fits), and no direct evidence of their demand was found |

## Actual differentiation

Fluency Check is the only tool found that lets one person, on any device, with no account and nothing recorded or uploaded, score a live reading word by word on any passage, then keep a printable marked record and a per-student history. Its differentiation is real but narrow, and it rests on two values the evidence hasn't shown to drive adoption: privacy and zero setup. Against a teacher who accepts an account, ReadingFluency.app is more capable for free. Against a teacher with a mandated platform or Wonders, it adds a step.

## Pricing expectations and business-model hypothesis

| Model | Evidence | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Permanently free | The norm at teacher level: free in Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace, free DIBELS materials and easyCBM Lite, ~35 free web tools, ReadingFluency.ai free for teachers [V] | **Yes**, and the only model Labs allows |
| Free individual, paid school | ReadingFluency.app, ReadingFluency.ai, easyCBM [V] | Needs accounts, agreements, and sales |
| One-time purchase | $1–$4 trackers, a $9.99 web app, $3.99 apps, with little visible demand [V] | Weak: the money is in passages, not tools |
| Freemium or paid teacher tier | $8 a month (ReadingFluency.app), $69.99 a year (easyCBM Deluxe), $9.99 a year (ReadFluent) [V] | Exists; uptake unknown; a paid tool pulls it into district approval (software requests, often an agreement even when no data is collected) |
| Institutional licensing | Dominant: about $1–$20 per student per year [V/S] | Needs standardized passages, norms, evidence, procurement, and agreements. Not this tool |

**Conclusion:** teachers expect this workflow to be free. Willingness to pay exists where tools come with standardized passages, norms, automation, or district data systems, and it's districts that pay. Teachers spend their own money mostly on printable content, about $895 a year on supplies on average (AdoptAClassroom.org's 2025 survey) [V]. Pricing would add procurement complexity and break Labs' rules. The pilot should test repeated use, not willingness to pay. If Fluency Check ever needed revenue, the evidence points to content (equivalent passage sets), not the tool, which would be a different product.

## Brand collision

- **UFLI Foundations "Fluency Check"** (high risk of confusion; same audience, same activity): the University of Florida Literacy Institute's one-minute WCPM unit checks for grades 1–2 are titled "Fluency Checks" [V].
- McGraw Hill's tool calls each attempt a "fluency check" [V].
- No FLUENCY CHECK trademark appeared in third-party listings [S]; the USPTO database wasn't searched [U].
- The name describes the activity, so it's hard to protect and unlikely to infringe, but it isn't distinctive. Keep the permanent Labs slug; a standalone would need a distinctive name and a trademark search first.

## Gaps
