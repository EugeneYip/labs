# Workflow research: how oral reading fluency is really monitored

Winner Validation Phase 1 for #016 Fluency Check (research 2026-10-10 and 11). Internal. Detailed evidence, with every source, is in `notes/workflow-notes.md` (procedures and practice) and `notes/pain-notes.md` (65 practitioner and market evidence items). Labels: **[V]** read on the source itself; **[S]** search snippet or secondary; **[U]** unverified or inference. Levels: **(a)** published procedure or official guidance, **(b)** common practice, **(c)** one person's or one school's account.

**Limits.** Reddit couldn't be read by any tool available (the research tools refuse it, and the built-in browser blocks it), and the main teacher forums (ProTeacher, A to Z Teacher Stuff, Well-Trained Mind) returned errors, so candid recent practitioner voices are under-represented, especially tutors and homeschool parents. The research threads' shared search allowance also ran out partway. Two scholarly sources are used and marked as not resolved through LibKey, which this session doesn't have.

## Bottom line

1. **The core routine is standard, and Fluency Check matches it** (a) [V]. Every major system uses a one-minute timed reading, a mark on each error, a bracket on the last word, and words correct per minute (WCPM) = words attempted − errors. The rules converge: a 3-second hesitation is an error (the examiner supplies the word), self-corrections within 3 seconds count as correct, insertions and repetitions are ignored, and skipped words and lines are errors. Fluency Check's rules match the DIBELS 8 text.
2. **In US public schools, the official checks belong to the district or state** (a) [V]. Screening, and often progress monitoring, runs on publisher passages inside systems the state or district chooses. Counts of states with approved screener lists range from 7 (ExcelinEd, 2024) to 42 (a vendor's 2026 tally), because definitions and dates differ. Iowa's 2026–27 rules also require approved progress-monitoring tools, with data entered into the state's system.
3. **The incumbents already put scoring on a device.** mCLASS, aimswebPlus, easyCBM, Acadience Learning Online, and McGraw Hill's tool inside the Wonders curriculum all let the teacher score live on a screen [V]. Paper users have free passages with running word counts printed on each line (DIBELS 8, Acadience, UFLI), so the arithmetic takes seconds.
4. **The real burden isn't the part Fluency Check automates.** Practitioners' pain is one-on-one time multiplied by caseload and frequency, plus managing the rest of the class during testing [V, many sources]. Scoring, recording, and graphing get little complaint.
5. **Valid progress monitoring needs equivalent passages and 6–8 or more data points** (a) [V]. Teacher-chosen passages vary enough in difficulty to hide weeks of growth. Fluency Check's chart is fine for an informal series, but it isn't a progress-monitoring system.

## 1. How often checks happen

| Use | Frequency | Level and source |
|---|---|---|
| Universal screening (benchmark), all K–3 students | Three times a year (fall, winter, spring) in most systems; at least twice a year in Massachusetts | (a) [V] DIBELS 8, Acadience, state guidance |
| Progress monitoring, Tier 2 | At least every two weeks (MTSS Center, 2025); at least monthly (IES practice guide, 2009); every 2–4 weeks for some-risk students (DIBELS 8) | (a) [V] |
| Progress monitoring, Tier 3 and intensive | At least weekly (MTSS Center, 2025; FastBridge); every two weeks for at-risk students (DIBELS 8); weekly once structured-literacy small groups start (Ohio, 2025) | (a) [V] |
| Special education (IEP goals) | Measured as the IEP specifies, with periodic progress reports (often quarterly); a resource teacher with a caseload of 20+ runs a monitoring day every other week | (a) [V] 34 CFR 300.320; (c) [V] |
| Curriculum-embedded checks | UFLI Foundations: one decodable passage per unit in grades 1–2, at least a week after the unit | (a/b) [V] |
| Tutors and homeschool families | Not measured anywhere found. Guidance aimed at them (Outschool, BJU Press) describes periodic timed readings and charting, frequency unstated | (b) [V]; frequency [U] |

## 2. The session

- **Length** (a) [V]: DIBELS 8 oral reading fluency is 60 seconds of reading; a progress-monitoring probe takes 1–2 minutes per student (Iowa); full screening batteries take 2–7 minutes per student by grade, about 5 for Acadience's three passages plus retell.
- **One minute or the whole passage** (a) [V]: official measures use a fixed 60 seconds. Fluency Check's whole-passage mode, scaled to a minute, is a convenience no official measure uses, though FastBridge reportedly adjusts readings under a minute [S].
- **Marking** (a) [V]: a slash through each error and a bracket after the last word on the examiner's copy; "SC" for self-corrections. With a device (mCLASS, easyCBM online, McGraw Hill), the teacher taps words while the student reads from a printed page.
- **WCPM and accuracy** (a) [V]: WCPM = words read − errors. Accuracy = correct ÷ attempted. DIBELS 8 and Acadience treat accuracy as a risk indicator with grade-specific cutoffs (96% or higher for core support from mid-grade 2 in DIBELS 8), while the running-record and reading-inventory tradition uses 95% and 90% as comfortable and challenging bands, which is what Fluency Check shows.
- **Materials** (a) [V]: student copy, examiner copy with cumulative word counts per line, timer, clipboard. Newsela now generates both copies, with running counts and a WCPM box, from any of its articles (2026) [V].
- **Who gives the checks** (scholarly survey of 251 K–3 educators in 39 states, Ozernov-Palchik et al. 2025, *Annals of Dyslexia*, open access; not resolved via LibKey): literacy specialists or coaches 36%, classroom teachers 25%, special-education teachers 16%, other roles including interventionists 15%. The same survey found most administrators had under three hours of training and only 12% strictly followed standardized protocols.

## 3. Passages: benchmark versus progress monitoring

- **Benchmark and progress-monitoring interpretations assume the publisher's own passages and procedures** (a) [V]. DIBELS treats any modified administration as invalid for its data system and benchmarks. NCII's tools chart rates progress-monitoring tools on alternate-form equivalence, and Iowa requires vendors to document how forms were made similar.
- **Teacher-chosen passages are noisy.** In a master's thesis (scholarly, not resolved via LibKey; snippet only), six passages leveled by readability formulas produced class means from 67.9 to 93.9 WCPM. Expected growth is about 1–2 WCPM a week, so text differences can hide several weeks of growth [U, inference].
- **Informal checks with other passages are legitimate when done carefully** (b) [V]. Hasbrouck's practitioner guidance allows passages picked from a book if two or three readings are averaged. Curriculum-based measurement began with passages from students' own textbooks, and compiled norms (Hasbrouck & Tindal, 2017) are used outside specific products, with care.
- **Licensing** (a) [V]: DIBELS 8 materials are licensed only to schools, districts, and multi-district agencies for internal educational use, may not be modified, and may be used electronically only as a static PDF; Amplify is the digital partner. Homeschool families and private tutors fall outside the licence, and typing DIBELS passages into another tool sits uneasily with it. UFLI's Fluency Checks are free to use, adapt, and share for non-commercial purposes with attribution.

## 4. After the score

- **Where results go** (a) [V]: into the district or state system (DIBELS Data System by hand, mCLASS automatically, Iowa's state system by hand for vendors without an integration).
- **Decisions** (a) [V]: NCII's four-point rule needs at least 3 weeks and 6 data points; trend lines need at least 4 weeks and 8 points; goals and baselines must come from the same tool. Platforms draw the graphs; NCII offers a free Excel graphing tool.
- **Uses**: grouping students (Massachusetts guidance), IEP progress reports (federal rule), data meetings, and family reports (Massachusetts sample letters; parent portals) [V].
- **Longitudinal history is essential for progress monitoring; rosters are essential for schools, convenient for individuals.** District platforms sync rosters; easyCBM's free edition has students typed by hand, up to 200 [V]. For an individual tutor with a handful of students, typed names work [U].

## 5. Devices

- District tools score on teacher devices (tablets, laptops) while students read print; group tests run on student Chromebooks or iPads [V].
- In the 2025 survey (scholarly, as above), 75% of respondents used digital screening formats and 15% paper; technical problems affected about 60% of sessions in lower-income schools against about 40% in higher-income ones.
- No verified data was found on iPad versus Chromebook shares in elementary classrooms, or on teachers' own phones during testing [U]. A research preprint (432 instructors, 2025; not resolved via LibKey) found 87% use personal devices for teaching.

## 6. Pain: what the evidence says

Details and sources: `notes/pain-notes.md` (65 items: 18 support the pain, 25 argue against it, 22 cut both ways).

**Real pains**
- One-on-one testing time, multiplied by caseload and frequency: consistent from 2010 to 2026, from K–2 teachers in screening windows, special-education teachers, and interventionists [V]. Most of that time is the reading itself and managing the class, which no scoring tool removes.
- Unreliable district platforms: mCLASS had outages in August, September, and October 2025, one during beginning-of-year testing [V].
- Teachers spend their own money on monitoring materials, but on passages: a $20 set of 60 progress-monitoring passages with examiner copies has 4,150 ratings; digital WCPM trackers mostly have 0–2 [V].
- Some distrust of automatic scoring for young readers: a teacher correcting Microsoft Reading Progress by hand; a union president on Amira; New Mexico's 2026 opt-outs over children's voice recordings [V].

**Thin pains**
- WCPM arithmetic, double entry, and graphing each have only scattered or old evidence. Graphs are wanted, but platforms draw them, or students plot their own as motivation [V].

**Counter-evidence**
- The same workflow already exists free or bundled: McGraw Hill's tool in Wonders (tap errors, tap last word, words per minute, graph; verified 2026-10-11), easyCBM's online tap-to-score, Microsoft Reading Progress with teachers' own passages, and teacher-built apps that show almost no traction [V].
- Paper is described as quick: reviewers call printed passage sets quick and easy and use them weekly [V].
- Tool fatigue: nearly two-thirds of educators reported tech fatigue (EdWeek Research Center, 2022) [V].
- Some educators distrust WCPM as a speed measure (Shanahan; Hasbrouck calls oral reading fluency a thermometer, not a diagnosis) [V].

## 7. What makes a teacher abandon a tool (from the evidence)

- It duplicates a mandated system, so scores must be typed in twice (DIBELS Data System entry; Iowa's state entry) [V].
- It fails at the wrong moment: outages in testing windows, crashes after updates, records that won't transfer [V].
- It scores wrongly, or the teacher can't trust it: automatic scoring of young readers, a calculator about 4 WPM off another one [V].
- It can't keep per-student records (the one review of a $9.99 local-only running-record app) [V].
- Setup is confusing (FastBridge groups) or it's one more app among 10–15 (vendor-sponsored survey) [V/S].
- Data disappears. Not observed in this evidence, but structural for any browser-only tool (Safari's tracking prevention may remove a site's storage after seven days of Safari use without interaction; ephemeral Chromebook sessions) [V on the mechanisms].

## 8. By role

| Role | Pain | Freedom to choose a tool | Checks how often |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–3 classroom teachers | High in screening windows, inside mandated platforms | Low for official checks; some for informal ones; their curriculum may already include a tool | 3 times a year plus some monitoring |
| Reading interventionists | High: monitoring eats intervention time | Moderate: extra monitoring may be their call; official monitoring often isn't | Weekly or every two weeks |
| Reading specialists and literacy coaches | Moderate; they give many screenings | Moderate; coaches influence other teachers | Screening windows; coaching cycles |
| Special-education teachers | Highest: caseloads, many IEP goals, data scrambles | Low to moderate: IEP data goes into district systems; they buy their own materials | Every two weeks; weekly for intensive needs |
| Private tutors | Little direct evidence (one tutor wanted the arithmetic done) | High: outside district licences and systems | Not measured |
| Homeschool parents | No evidence found | High | Not measured |

The workflow evidence argues against classroom teachers as a first audience and points to roles outside mandated systems, where direct evidence of pain is thinnest. That tension is the central finding of this phase.
