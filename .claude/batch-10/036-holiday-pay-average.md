# #036 Holiday Pay Average

Dossier for Batch 10's first experiment. Chosen 2026-10-14 (`CANDIDATES.md`, "Decision for #036"); built on `wip/036-holiday-pay-average`.

## The job

A UK small employer (or their bookkeeper or payroll clerk) has a worker whose pay or hours vary, and the worker has booked leave. The holiday pay for that leave is "a week's pay" worked out from the worker's last 52 weeks of pay, with some weeks skipped and earlier ones brought in, never more than 104 weeks back (Working Time Regulations 1998 reg 16, applying the Employment Rights Act 1996 ss.221–224). Payroll products often leave this to the employer (Xero declined it in November 2025), and the free calculators take a total you've already worked out. Since 6 April 2026 the employer must keep records adequate to show the holiday pay was right, for six years (reg 16B). Workers checking their own holiday pay face the same rule.

## Scope (first version)

- **Where:** Great Britain (England, Scotland, Wales), the rules in force in October 2026. Northern Ireland has its own regulations; the page says so.
- **Three ways a worker's week's pay is worked out**, chosen first:
  1. **No normal working hours** (zero-hours, casual, most irregular-hours workers; ERA s.224): the average weekly pay over the reference period. Weeks of paid holiday count; all pay in a week counts.
  2. **Normal working hours, but pay varies with the work** (piece rates, commission tied to output, or hours that vary by rota; ss.221(3), 222, 223): an average hourly rate from the hours worked and the pay for them, times the normal weekly hours. Weeks with no work, and holiday weeks, are left out.
  3. **Normal working hours and set basic pay, plus extras** (regular overtime, commission, shift and status payments; s.221(2) with reg 16(3ZA)–(3ZG)): the basic weekly pay plus the average weekly extras over the relevant period, which leaves out any week with no pay, sick leave, or statutory family leave. The figure is for the 4 weeks under reg 13; for the extra 1.6 weeks (reg 13A) basic pay is the minimum, and paying the same figure is allowed.
- **The week table:** the weeks are listed back from the reference period's end, each with what happened (worked; no work and no pay; off sick on statutory sick pay or nothing; statutory family leave; paid holiday; unpaid leave; not yet employed) and the pay (and hours, where the method needs them). Typed, or pasted from a spreadsheet (week-ending dates optional).
- **The result:** the reference period's dates, the weeks used and skipped with each one's reason, the totals, the week's pay to the penny (and the unrounded figure), and where it applies the hourly rate for irregular-hours and part-year workers' leave (reg 16(1A): the week's pay ÷ the average weekly hours in the weeks used). A page to print or save as the record, and the week table as a spreadsheet file.
- **Decides nothing that is the employer's judgment:** which payments count (whether overtime is "regular", whether a bonus is linked to the job) is the employer's input, said plainly; the page applies the selection and the arithmetic.
- **Kept:** the current working in this browser (`labs:holiday-pay-average`) so a reload doesn't lose typed weeks, with a control to clear it; nothing is sent anywhere.
- **Left out:** monthly-paid apportionment by hours (the guidance's method is explained, the weekly figures are entered), the 12.07% accrual (GOV.UK's own calculator does entitlement), rolled-up holiday pay, payments in lieu on leaving, Northern Ireland, several workers at once.

## The rules as the page applies them

- **The calculation date** is the first day of the leave. **A week** ends on Saturday, unless the worker is paid weekly by weeks ending on another day (reg 16(3B), ERA s.235). **The reference period ends** with the week ending on the calculation date if that is the last day of a week, otherwise with the last complete week before it (s.224(2), reg 16(3ZD)). Nothing before the 104 weeks ending with that week counts (reg 16(3)(f), (3ZG)).
- **Weeks left out, by what happened:** no work and no pay; off sick for any of the week (statutory sick pay or nothing; GOV.UK 2024 and reg 16(3ZE)); statutory family leave (maternity, paternity, adoption, shared parental, parental bereavement, neonatal care, unpaid parental, carer's: "statutory leave", ERA Parts 8 and 8B); unpaid leave; not yet employed (and every week before). A week marked worked or holiday with no pay entered stops the count until it's filled in, rather than being skipped silently.
- **Method 1, no normal hours (s.224):** paid holiday weeks count (GOV.UK 2020: "For workers without regular hours, a week of holiday pay is included"); the week's pay is the total pay of the weeks used divided by their number. For irregular-hours and part-year workers taking leave in hours, the hourly rate is the week's pay divided by the average weekly hours worked in the weeks used (reg 16(1A)); paid holiday weeks add pay but no hours worked.
- **Method 2, normal hours with varying pay (ss.221(3), 222, 223):** holiday weeks are left out (no hours worked; GOV.UK 2020, its Table 9); the average hourly rate is the pay for work divided by the hours worked in the weeks used; the week's pay is that rate times the normal weekly hours, or for hours that vary by rota, times the average weekly hours in the weeks used.
- **Method 3, set basic pay plus extras (s.221(2), reg 16(3ZA)–(3ZG)):** the average weekly extras over the relevant period (weeks with any sick or statutory leave, or no pay, left out; earlier weeks brought in to 104); paid holiday weeks are left out by default, the higher of the two readings (the regulations don't name annual leave among the weeks to leave out), with a choice to count them.
- **Where the guidance is specific, the page follows it; where the regulations leave a choice, it says which reading it took** (and for paid holiday weeks in the extras' average, shows both figures). An earlier draft claimed it always took the higher reading; the audit (D-14) showed that isn't always so, so the claim was dropped.
- **Rounding:** money in whole pence, hours in whole seconds, sums exact; the result rounded to the nearest penny (half up), with the unrounded figure shown.

## Principal failure modes, and how each is handled and checked

1. **The wrong end week.** The calculation date on the week's last day (that week counts), a custom week end, month and year ends, leap days, clock changes. Dates are calendar dates (`YYYY-MM-DD`) handled as whole days in UTC, never local times. *Check:* every leave date over three years against every week-end day, against a brute-force walk through the days; the GOV.UK shift example (leave 26 October 2020: week 1 is 18–24 October).
2. **The wrong weeks used.** Each status's rule above, per method; the 104-week limit; employment starting inside the window; fewer than 52; none at all (the "fairly represents" message, reg 16(3A)). *Check:* GOV.UK's tables (Amy 75 paid of 104 → 52; Ben 45 → 45; Rachel 20; the irregular-hours worker's 59 weeks with 7 unpaid); random histories against a Python re-implementation; invariants (only eligible weeks used, the most recent ones, never more than 52, never beyond 104).
3. **Wrong arithmetic.** Floating-point drift, rounding, zero hours (no division by zero), very large figures. *Check:* integer pence and seconds throughout; exact totals; the official examples recomputed; random cases against Python's `fractions`.
4. **Silent gaps.** An empty pay cell inside the weeks needed must stop the result, naming the week, not be skipped. *Check:* model cases and the browser flow.
5. **Figures moving to the wrong week when the leave date changes.** Weeks are kept by their week-ending date, not by row, so moving the leave date shifts the rows and the figures stay with their dates; changing the week-end day re-aligns them in order, with a notice. *Check:* model and browser.
6. **Pasting.** Pound signs, commas, spaces, minus signs, `h:mm` hours, tabs or commas between columns, UK dates (`dd/mm/yyyy`, `2020-10-24`, `24 Oct 2020`), dates that aren't week ends (put in the week containing them), two lines for one week (refused, naming the week), newest-first or oldest-first lists without dates, thousands of lines (the first 104 weeks taken, the rest refused with a count). *Check:* a paste fixture set and the browser flow.
7. **Misleading output.** A provisional figure shown as final; the employer's judgments (which payments count) presented as the page's; Northern Ireland; the 1.6 weeks. *Check:* the wording review in the audit.
8. **Storage and tabs.** Saved state damaged, refused, or changed by another tab (`useOtherTabs`, `NotSaving`). *Check:* damaged-storage fuzz, refused storage, two tabs.
9. **Fast typing.** Typing in pay cells and pressing Enter at speed (Comp Desk): raw strings kept as typed, functional updates, no delayed focus moves. *Check:* the browser flow types at full speed.
10. **Accessibility.** 104 rows of inputs: labels naming the week, Up and Down arrows and Enter moving between rows in the same column, a skip link to the result, the result announced politely, 200% text, phone width. *Check:* axe, keyboard pass, Tab count noted.
11. **Print.** The working on paper: every week with its fate and reason, the totals, the result, the rules applied. *Check:* a printed PDF read back.

## Evidence plan

- **Official worked examples recomputed:** GOV.UK 2020 and 2024 (the irregular-hours worker's 59 weeks, £12,040 over 52; the monthly-paid worker's 52 weekly figures; the leaver's 52 weeks at three rates, £394.62; the reference-period cases), the Insolvency Service's (Sam £250; Robin's hours), Acas's (Mo, Corey). Where an official example's own arithmetic slips, the test says so and checks against the recomputation.
- **A second implementation in Python** (`.claude/qa/holiday-pay-check.py`), written from the rules above, not from the TypeScript, run on the official examples and on thousands of random week histories exported by the TypeScript test.
- **Brute force:** the end week by walking days; the selection by a direct definition (filter, take the most recent 52 within 104).
- **Limits:** 104 weeks with every status; pastes of 10,000 lines and 1 MB; pay up to £1 million a week; hours up to 168.
