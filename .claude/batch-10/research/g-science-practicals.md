# Thread G: science practical work and amateur science (Batch 10, discovery round 2)

One bounded purpose: real, recurring jobs in science practical work and amateur science where a set method (an exam board's specification, a lab's standard method, a citizen-science protocol) creates data handling that people do badly or slowly today, and a static, no-account browser tool could do it materially better. Budget: 8 web searches, 6 page reads. Notes written as the thread goes, so this file may stop mid-way.

Evidence labels: *verified* (official or primary page seen), *source claim* (a page says so), *inference* (judgment).

## Ranked summary

Budget used: 8 searches (two more were refused before running: Reddit and Stack Exchange block the crawler) and 6 page reads. Three of the reads were PDFs that WebFetch couldn't parse but saved; their text was read locally with macOS PDFKit, with nothing else downloaded.

1. **Lead (moderate): best and worst lines through error bars, and the gradient's uncertainty, for physics practical write-ups** (AQA, OCR, the IB, from memory Cambridge 9702, and first-year university labs; not Edexcel). The method is set (AQA's handbook, *verified*: half-range bars, the "steepest or shallowest" line, |best − worst| / best × 100%, and the same for the intercept). The friction is long-standing: nine Student Room threads ask how (their thread numbers suggest about 2012 to 2020, *inference*), and every general tool needs a workaround. Excel needs hand-built series. Excel's LINEST ignores the bars: on a 2023 paper's data its slope uncertainty was about a quarter of the max/min answer (*verified*). Vernier sells hand-dragged lines in Graphical Analysis Pro, behind a subscription (*verified*). The wedge is the exact global extremes, computed instantly as a small linear programme; a clear statement when no line passes through every bar; the board's convention named; the whole chain from repeats to the final value, printed; and it runs on Chromebooks and phones. **Against:** the exams test these lines drawn by hand; AQA says a calculator meets the endorsement; Edexcel expects no error bars; and AQA's worst line is a judgment. **Deciding fact, unchecked:** a 2023 IOP paper names beepboopmachines.com's "IB physics graphing tool" as computing these lines. If it's live, free and good, this lead drops to weak.
2. **Weak: NO2 diffusion-tube results for community groups.** The steps are set by Defra's LAQM.TG(22), but Defra's free Diffusion Tube Data Processing Tool (v6.1, May 2026, *verified*) already does all of them. Annualisation needs background data a static page can't keep current, and the work follows #035's pattern.
3. **Weak, unsearched:** IMO meteor counts (tiny audience, seasonal); manual weather observers' monthly returns (records span years, official systems need accounts).
4. **Rejected:** the endorsement's record (shared, multi-year records); technicians' preparation (shared requisitions, members-only CLEAPSS, the dilution calculators the brief rules out); biology and geography statistical tests, titrations and significant figures (served free); other amateur astronomy, radio and water testing (free desktop tools, or schemes' own apps behind accounts).

## Searches and reads

### Search 1: free tools for best and worst lines through error bars (kill test for lead 1)

Query: "online tool error bars line of best fit worst fit line uncertainty in gradient A level physics".
- Results were revision notes only, nine of them Save My Exams pages for AQA, OCR and IB (for example https://www.savemyexams.com/a-level/physics/aqa/17/revision-notes/1-measurements-and-their-errors/1-2-limitation-of-physical-measurements/1-2-3-determining-uncertainties-from-graphs/ and https://savemyexams.com/dp/physics/hl/25/revision-notes/tools/processing-uncertainties/determining-uncertainties-from-graphs), plus TutorChase (https://www.tutorchase.com/notes/a-level-ocr/physics/3-2-5-graphical-treatment-lines-and-error-analysis). No tool appeared on the first page (*source claim*, search results).
- The method as the revision notes give it (*source claim*): error bars show each point's absolute uncertainty; the worst line is "the steepest or shallowest" line that still passes within all the error bars; the gradient's percentage uncertainty is |best − worst| / best × 100 (AQA-style example: best 0.25, worst 0.28, 12%); the IB pages use half the range of the maximum and minimum gradients instead. So the convention differs by board (*source claim*; to check against the boards' own handbooks).
- *Inference:* the same result as Batch 08's one search, so the absence of a tool on page one isn't yet evidence of a gap; the incumbents to test are general graphing tools (Vernier Graphical Analysis, Desmos, Excel, Google Sheets, GeoGebra), not a dedicated "worst line" site.

### Search 2: the strongest general incumbent, Vernier (kill test for lead 1)

Query: "Vernier Graphical Analysis error bars manual fit maximum minimum slope uncertainty".
- Vernier's own Tech Info Library (*source claim*, search summary of https://www.vernier.com/til/19724, not read): a **Manual Fit** arrived in Graphical Analysis **Pro** 6.0 (April 2024) and "a Pro subscription is needed"; it doesn't optimise, you drag the line by its handles and the slope, intercept and RMSE update; up to three manual lines at once, suggested for a best line plus lines of maximum and minimum slope "to graphically estimate the uncertainty of the slope value".
- Vernier's Logger Pro article (*source claim*, search summary of https://www.vernier.com/til/3784, not read): with error bars shown, place the line from the top of the left-most error bar to the bottom of the right-most for the minimum slope, the reverse for the maximum; it notes "some specific curricula use an alternate technique" with only two points; δm = |m_max − m_min| / 2 as "a common convention".
- University lab manuals teach the same by hand (Bowdoin, https://courses.bowdoin.edu/physics-1140-lab-manual/physics-1140-graphical-analysis-for-a-straight-line-graph/; Minnesota appendix, https://groups.spa.umn.edu/physed/Research/Lab%20Manuals/1202/Appendix%20C.pdf) (*source claim*, titles and summary only).
- *Inference:* the market leader for school data graphing sells the max/min-line job as a paid, drag-it-yourself feature, and its desktop predecessor's recipe (corner to corner of the end bars) is not the same as the AQA-style "steepest or shallowest line through all the error bars". Neither computes the extreme lines; the student still places them by eye. That's a possible wedge: computing the exact steepest and shallowest lines that pass through every bar, under the board's convention. Still to check: whether free tools (Desmos, GeoGebra, Excel, Google Sheets) or the free Graphical Analysis already do it, and what the boards themselves ask for.

### Read 1: AQA's own handbook (primary source for lead 1)

AQA, "Required practical handbook", AS and A-level Physics (7407, 7408), Version 2.1, 149 pages: https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/physics/AQA-7407-7408-PHBK.PDF. WebFetch couldn't parse the PDF but saved it; its text was read locally with macOS PDFKit (no other download). All *verified* from that text:
- **Repeats** (p.43): "If measurements are repeated, the uncertainty can be calculated by finding half the range of the measured values" (worked example: 1.23, 1.32, 1.27, 1.22 m gives (1.26 ± 0.05) m). Percentage uncertainty = uncertainty / value × 100%, or / mean value for repeats; examples show the rounding judgment (0.355 ± 0.015 mm "could be quoted as such or recorded as 0.36 ±0.02 mm"; "5% or 6% would be acceptable").
- **Error bars** (p.44): "There are a number of ways to draw error bars"; an acceptable simple method: plot the point at the mean, take the range "ignoring any anomalies", and draw bars of half the range either side.
- **The gradient's uncertainty** (p.45): "two lines should be drawn on the graph. One should be the 'best' line of best fit. The second line should be the steepest or shallowest gradient line of best fit possible from the data"; percentage uncertainty = |best gradient − worst gradient| / best gradient × 100%, and "in the same way" for the y-intercept. Note: AQA's own wording doesn't say "through all the error bars"; the revision notes in Search 1 do.
- **Combining** (p.46): add absolute uncertainties for sums and differences, add percentages for products and quotients, multiply for powers; "Uncertainties in trigonometric and logarithmic functions will not be tested".
- **Graphing** (p.47–48): "Graphing skills can be assessed both in written papers ... and by the teacher during the assessment of the endorsement"; axes labelled quantity/unit with a solidus; points as crosses; "Error bars, standard deviation and ranges can take the place of data points where appropriate"; in exams plots cover at least half the grid.
- **Software in the endorsement:** CPAC 5a "Uses appropriate software and/or tools to process data, carry out research and report findings" (p.24); apparatus and technique ATk "Use ICT such as computer modelling, or data logger with a variety of sensors to collect data, or use of software to process data" (p.30-ish, and in the practicals). But AQA's FAQ (p.140) answers "Must they use software to pass this, or would drawing graphs by hand, using calculators to perform calculations suffice?" with "A calculator is a tool and so, to gain evidence towards the pass for CPAC 5 this would suffice", adding that processing "using dataloggers or Excel for example, that will be a good experience".
- **Endorsement records** (p.32–33): teachers keep records of each practical, the criteria assessed, attendance, who met which criterion, and dated student work, "on tracking spreadsheets", "teacher mark books", "sheets stuck at the front of students' lab books", "in computer-based systems".
- **Uncertainty in practical work** (p.44): "Teachers will decide on each occasion what acceptable uncertainty values are"; uncertainties feed CPAC 2, 4 (significant figures in calculated values) and 5 (does the true value lie within the range).
- *Inference:* the method is set and examined, but loosely: AQA allows several error-bar methods, leaves acceptable uncertainties to the teacher, and defines the worst line as the steepest or shallowest "possible from the data", a judgment, not an algorithm. Software is optional for the endorsement and hand-drawn graphs on paper are what the written papers examine. That's the strongest reason not to build so far: the assessed skill is drawing these lines by hand, so a tool that draws them is at best a checker or a write-up aid. The endorsement record is a class-wide, two-year, multi-teacher record (tracking spreadsheets, mark books), which fails Labs' static, one-browser limit.

### Read 3: Pearson Edexcel's "Uncertainties and practical work" (a second board, lead 1)

https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/A%20Level/Physics/2015/Specification%20and%20sample%20assessments/Appendix%2010%20Updated.pdf (6 pages; the file's metadata says "Appendix 10 Updated FINAL", 15 November 2017). ### Search 7: community NO2 diffusion-tube surveys (lead 2, a citizen-science protocol)

Why this lead: community groups, parish councils and school campaigns put out NO2 diffusion tubes, get a lab's monthly results, and want to know whether a street exceeds the 40 µg/m³ annual mean objective. The set method (Defra's LAQM.TG(22)) has steps a raw comparison skips: national or local bias adjustment, annualisation when fewer months were captured, and fall-off with distance to the nearest relevant exposure. Query: "community group NO2 diffusion tube results bias adjustment annualisation distance correction citizen science guidance spreadsheet".
- **The official tool:** Defra's Diffusion Tube Data Processing Tool (https://laqm.defra.gov.uk/aq_reporting/diffusion-tube-data-processing-tool/): per the search summary, v6.1 released May 2026, an XLSB workbook of 7.8 MB, doing annualisation (for data capture of 3 to 8 months), bias adjustment, and fall-off with distance, with an output table for upload to the LAQM Portal; detail in LAQM.TG(22) (*source claim*). An older local-bias spreadsheet (February 2011) calculates a co-location factor with a 95% interval (https://laqm.defra.gov.uk/aq_reporting/local-bias/, *source claim*).
- **Community practice:** a parish council's own summer report (Bray Parish Council, 2022, https://brayparishcouncil.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Bray-Parish-Council-AQA-Summer-Report-Aug-2022-1.pdf); Mapping for Change's step-by-step toolkit with printable pen-and-paper templates (2021, https://mappingforchange.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Toolkit.pdf); the Clean Cities Campaign and ISGlobal protocol for school and community groups (2024, https://cleancitiescampaign.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/PROTOCOL-FOR-MONITORING-AIR-QUALITY-VF-1.pdf); Barcelona's xAire campaign, calibrated and annualised against reference stations (MethodsX, doi 10.1016/j.mex.2021.101475) (*source claim*, titles and summary).
- No community-specific tool combining the steps turned up (*source claim*, search summary).
- *Inference:* the official, free tool already does every step, but as a 7.8 MB Excel binary built for council officers and their portal; the community gap is know-how, not arithmetic. Annualisation needs background continuous-monitor data for the same months, which a static page can't keep current; the national bias factors change every year (rules that move); and "lab results against an objective, written up as a report" is close to #035's pattern. Weak unless the tool's own page shows it's unusable by a community group. One read to check.

### Read 6: the IOP paper's full text (lead 1's incumbents and the method's divergence)

https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/2653/1/012015/pdf (7 pages; WebFetch couldn't parse it, and its text was read locally with PDFKit as in Read 1). *Verified* from that text:
- **An existing web tool:** "Only IB physics graphing tool on website http://beepboopmachines.com is able to fix these lines by putting experimental x-data, y-data and information of error bars into spreadsheet. Linear equations of the lines are shown with graphing." Its figures 1 and 2 are screenshots of that tool. Whether it's still live in 2026, free, usable on a phone, or handles x error bars and the A-level conventions is **not checked** (budget spent).
- **The authors' own app** is Python with Streamlit "run on Visual Studio Code", taking a CSV of x, y and y-error; so it isn't a public site. Its method is an incremental search (steps of 0.01 or 0.001) that the authors say can't guarantee "the global optimal solution" and "tends to be computationally slow"; when no line passes through every bar it falls back to lines through the edges of chosen bars.
- **The methods disagree in practice:** their Table 1 gives the slope's uncertainty for one dataset as 2.789 (their app), 3.565 (the IB graphing tool) and 0.863 (Excel's LINEST, which "does not consider error bars"); for another, 0.056, 0.057 and 0.044. The IB tool's lines "are drawn to cover all data points resulting in high gradients".
- The paper says these lines "are difficult to draw without putting an error bar on experimental data points", and "it is hard to exactly identify which lines have minimum and maximum slopes even if there are drawing straight lines pass though error bars".
- *Inference:* this confirms the job and its difficulty from a third country's school physics, shows that a general tool's answer (LINEST) can be a quarter of the max/min answer, and names a direct incumbent (beepboopmachines.com) that the main session must check before any decision. The exact answer is a small linear programme (for a given sign of slope, "the line passes through every bar, or every error rectangle" is a set of linear inequalities in slope and intercept), so a Labs tool could give the global extremes instantly where the paper's search can't.

## Lead 1 in full: best and worst lines through error bars, and the gradient's uncertainty

- **A. Audience:** A-level physics students on AQA (7407/7408, *verified*), OCR (*source claim*, examiners' report title) and, from memory, Cambridge International 9702 (*inference*, not checked); IB Diploma physics students in lab reports and the Internal Assessment (*source claim*, revision notes); first-year university lab students taught max/min slopes (Bowdoin, Minnesota, *source claim*); their teachers, who set the convention and check the write-ups. **Not** Pearson Edexcel A-level physics, which has "no expectation that error bars should be added to graphs" (*verified*, Read 3).
- **B. Immediate job:** just before, a results table from a practical (repeats of each reading, for example times for g by free fall, or V and t for a capacitor's discharge plotted as ln V). After, they need the means with half-range uncertainties, the linearised quantities with propagated uncertainties, a graph with error bars, the best line and the steepest and shallowest acceptable lines, the gradient (and intercept) with its uncertainty under their board's rule, and the final value (for example g from the gradient) with its uncertainty compared with the accepted value, written into the lab book or report (*verified* as AQA's sequence, Read 1).
- **C. Why it matters:** recurring (every quantitative practical over two years; AQA's required practicals, OCR's PAGs, IB labs and the IA); rule-made (AQA's handbook, *verified*); graphing assessed by the teacher for the endorsement as well as in the papers (*verified*); error-prone (a decade of Student Room threads, a forgotten division by the best value "the difference between 1% and 30%", convention confusion, *source claim*); slow in general software (Excel needs extra series per graph, "no tool to create max/min lines in Excel" per Southampton; Google Sheets plus ChatGPT guides in 2025; teachers' videos, *source claim*); and general tools give a different answer (LINEST's slope uncertainty about a quarter of the max/min one on the paper's data, *verified*).
- **D. Current alternatives:** pencil, ruler and graph paper (the examined method); Excel's custom error bars plus hand-built series through end-bar corners (Southampton L4labs); Google Sheets workarounds; Desmos segments and sliders; Vernier Graphical Analysis Pro's Manual Fit (paid subscription, April 2024, placed by hand, *verified*); Logger Pro's Moveable Fit (paid desktop, *source claim*); LINEST (ignores error bars, *verified* via the paper); beepboopmachines.com's IB physics graphing tool (*source claim* from a 2023 paper, unchecked); revision notes (Save My Exams and others).
- **E. Strongest incumbent:** for the computation itself, beepboopmachines.com's IB physics graphing tool, if it's still live and free (the deciding unknown). For schools that pay, Vernier Graphical Analysis Pro. For everyone, paper, which is what the exams test.
- **F. Labs wedge:** the exact steepest and shallowest lines through every error bar, or every error rectangle when x is uncertain too, computed as a small linear programme (global, instant; the paper's own search admits it isn't); a plain statement when no straight line passes through every bar (a finding about the data or the bars), with the end-corner method offered as the named fallback; the board's convention named and followed (AQA |best − worst| / best × 100%; IB half the difference; the others shown), with the working printed; the whole chain from repeats to the final value with its uncertainty and the comparison with the accepted value; a checker mode for hand-drawn lines (type your two gradients, see whether they're within the extremes); no account or install, on school Chromebooks and phones, offline after a first visit.
- **G. Technical fit:** good. Static, local, tiny data (a handful of points), an SVG graph, a print page. Independent evidence is easy: brute-force grid search over slope and intercept, a Python re-check, the boards' worked examples, and the paper's published numbers where its data can be read off.
- **H. Strongest reasons not to build (searched for):** (1) the papers examine these lines drawn by hand, and AQA says a calculator suffices for the endorsement's software criterion (*verified*), so the tool is a checker and write-up aid, which some teachers will see as a shortcut; (2) Edexcel, a large A-level board, doesn't ask for error bars at all (*verified*); (3) the conventions differ and AQA's own worst line is a judgment, "possible from the data", not "through every bar" (*verified*), so an exact answer must be shown as one named convention, never as the board's verdict; (4) a free web tool that computes these lines may already exist (beepboopmachines.com, *source claim*, unchecked); (5) Batch 08 called the topic crowded, though it looked at revision notes, not tools.
- **Thread verdict: lead (moderate),** on one deciding fact: whether beepboopmachines.com's IB physics graphing tool is live, free, and does the job well (both error bars, the A-level conventions, the intercept, the working, phone width). If it does, this drops to weak; if it's gone or IB-only and opaque, the wedge above stands. Also worth reading in the main session before a decision: OCR's practical skills handbook or examiners' report (https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/538040-examiners-report-practical-skills-in-physics.pdf) for OCR's wording, and a Cambridge 9702 mark scheme for "worst acceptable line".
