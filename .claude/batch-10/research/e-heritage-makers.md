## Research log

### Lead 1: planning a collection shift (seed)

- **Search 1** (library collection shift planning spreadsheet calculator linear feet growth space per shelf shift map) (*source claim*, search summary):
  - The method is published and arithmetic: University of Oregon's "Planning Shifts of Collections" page (https://pages.uoregon.edu/chappell/shift/spreadsheet.html): count shelves added × shelf length (example 35 in) + existing growth space; total growth space ÷ final shelf count = average space to leave per shelf; cross-check: total inches of books ÷ final shelves, subtracted from shelf length.
  - C&RL News articles (1990s) lay out the spreadsheet: shelves and empty inches per class → linear feet, projected forward by the cataloguing department's volumes-added estimate; split big LC classes into subunits (QA, QB, QC) (https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/19472/22855; https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/20586/25070: "a spreadsheet is the right tool"; inputs: collection in linear inches, annual growth, shelf length, number of shelves in the new location).
  - Rules of thumb: shelves about 80% full; 20% growth allowance needed 23 extra sections in one example (https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/26427/34367). Pittsburgh assessment (Library Assessment Conference 2022, Koenig, "Finding a Home for A–Z", https://www.libraryassessment.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/71-Koenig-Finding-a-Home-for-A–Z.pdf): a buffer in the mapping was critical for inventory anomalies; avoid splitting LC classes between floors.
  - Existing tools: Harvard Library Lab's CollectionShift (estimates linear feet for an LC range from LibraryCloud bibliographic data, page counts from MARC 300, converts to person-hours; LC only; a 2011-12 grant project) (https://osc.hul.harvard.edu/liblab/sites/default/files/shiftintoolreport4.pdf); Fortriede, *Moving Your Library* (ALA) ships spreadsheet templates (ACRL review).
  - *Inference*: the job is real, method-driven and arithmetic, and done in spreadsheets. Still to test: how often a given library does it (one-off per move vs recurring "shifting" as growth fills shelves), whether a free modern tool exists, and whether the valuable artifact (a shift map per shelf/section with start call numbers) needs data a static tool can't get (call-number boundaries from the catalogue, or measurement on site).

- **Search 2** (librarian "shifting" collection: where each shelf starts, inches per shelf, tool or spreadsheet, forums) (*source claim*, search summary): no ready-made online shift calculator surfaced in two searches. SERIALST list thread "Space for serials growth" (February 2006, about ten replies, https://lists.simplelists.com/serialst/msg/11682865/): a librarian's spreadsheet with each title's current inches and projected growth, and a formula giving where each title begins and ends on numbered shelves (e.g. starts at shelf 7.46, ends at 8.75); removable labels on each range so staff can work anywhere; "accurate measuring is essential"; students measured with a tape and a laptop.
  - *Inference*: the method is a running total of measured inches plus growth, divided into shelf lengths. Practitioners build it themselves in spreadsheets each time; the evidence is old (1990s articles, a 2006 list thread) and no modern free tool has turned up. The hard part on the day is measuring and moving, not the division.

- **Read 1** (Koenig and Webster, University of Pittsburgh, Library Assessment Conference 2022 poster, https://www.libraryassessment.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/71-Koenig-Finding-a-Home-for-A–Z.pdf; WebFetch couldn't parse the PDF, so the text was taken from the fetched copy with a small local script) (*verified*, the poster's own text):
  - Hillman Library renovation, nearly 100,000 linear feet; collection removed from a floor early (spring 2022); had to "map and estimate final collection footprints" and tell the moving contractor how much would move and where.
  - Rules: shelves no more than 75–80% full; buffer by call-number range from recent acquisitions; LC class order, no class split between floors; contractors told which shelves hold which ranges.
  - Method: Alma Analytics inventory reports and a Tableau model → crosstab spreadsheet; XLOOKUP; linear feet estimated at 0.09 ft per book (about 11 per foot, "may be too high in some areas"); new adds × 3 for three years' growth; total ÷ 2 to 2.37 to get shelves needed per class; then classes mapped across the counted shelves, giving "targeted start and end ranges for call numbers for the movers". Empty shelves built in across sections and ranges; each class graded low/medium/high/very high growth for the contractor's fill rates.
  - Their conclusion: "building a buffer space in the mapping was critical"; inventory data "messy and incomplete" (lost items listed, unlisted items on the shelf); all techniques "can utilize freely available tools like spreadsheets".
  - *Inference*: a large library does this with analytics, Tableau and a spreadsheet, once per renovation or move; the hard parts are the inventory's errors and the moving itself. A static tool would replace the last spreadsheet step (shelves needed per class and start points per shelf) and add a printable map and labels; the item-count route means reading a catalogue export (the file-parsing cluster to avoid), and the tape-measure route is the small-library case.

- **Verdict so far on lead 1: weak** (*inference*, to settle at the end): the method is real and rule-bound (fill 75–80%, growth by class, no split classes, start and end call numbers per shelf), and no free tool turned up in two searches; but it's mostly a one-off per move or renovation, done by people already in spreadsheets (and in big libraries with analytics), and the hard parts (measuring, inventory errors, moving) stay manual.

### Lead 2: fragrance allergen labelling for small cosmetics makers (seed)

- **Search 3** (fragrance allergen calculator, 2023/1545, soap makers, 0.001% / 0.01%, free calculator, UK position) (*source claim*, search summary and snippets):
  - EU rule: allergens named individually above 0.001% in leave-on and 0.01% in rinse-off products; 2023/1545 takes the list from 24 to more than 80 substances and groups (sources differ on the count) (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1545; https://ecomundo.eu/en/blog/fragrance-allergen-labelling; https://www.personalcareinsights.com/news/eu-fragrance-allergen-labeling-deadline.html). Sell-through of non-compliant products placed earlier until 31 July 2028 (the summary garbled the first date; the new list applies to products placed on the market from 31 July 2026, *source claim* from memory of the regulation, not re-read).
  - UK: Great Britain has not adopted the expanded list; it still uses the older 24 (https://euverify.com/resource/eu-uk-fragrance-allergen-rules/, "no confirmed adoption ... at time being"); Northern Ireland follows the EU. So a GB maker selling into NI or the EU handles two lists.
  - Calculators: the results were dominated by spam "fragrance calculator" pages about fragrance load (how much oil for scent strength), none checking allergen limits. A Soap Making Forum thread title shows makers struggling to read suppliers' EU allergen sheets (https://soapmakingforum.com/threads/how-to-read-this-eu-allergen-sheet-and-safety-data-sheet-to-know-which-aromachem-s-is-the-offender.94388).
  - *Inference*: the computation is simple (each allergen's % in each oil × the oil's usage %, summed over oils, compared with the threshold for the product type), but the first search didn't find the incumbents I'd expect (suppliers' spreadsheets, safety assessors' outputs, makers' groups). One more search for those.

- **Search 4** ("allergen calculator", cosmetic or soap, usage rate, label, EU allergens, spreadsheet, supplier, safety assessor) (*source claim*, search summary and snippets): no dedicated free allergen calculator surfaced in a second search either.
  - Data comes per oil from suppliers: Bramble Berry's per-fragrance "IFRA / EU / Prop 65" sheets carry a 26-allergen section and state the thresholds (e.g. https://www.brambleberry.com/on/demandware.static/-/Sites-brambleberry-master-catalog/default/dwb89b120b/specs/ifra_eu_prop65/ifra_eu_prop65_V000350_ORANGE_BLOSSOM_CANDLE_AND_SOAP_FRAGRANCE.pdf); AFI supplies EU allergen reports for all its fragrances (https://info.afi-usa.com/eu-allergen-new/). Still the old 26 in these US sheets.
  - Method: allergen % in the oil × the oil's usage % in the product, summed (Registrar Corp, https://www.registrarcorp.com/blog/cosmetics/cosmetic-regulations/perfume-allergens-cosmetic-products). Makers confuse the IFRA usage limit with the allergen threshold (Soap Making Forum, https://www.soapmakingforum.com/threads/calculating-fragrance-oil-percentage.61434/).
  - Errors are common: the Danish EPA's survey of liquid hand soaps found 6 of 15 with contact allergens above 0.01% not on the label (2006, https://www2.mst.dk/Udgiv/publications/2006/87-7052-062-3/html/sum_eng.htm).
  - The UK small-maker guide (Low Impact, "Selling soap", https://cms.lowimpact.org/wp-content/uploads/26_selling_soap.pdf) sends makers to their safety assessor and supplier for the allergens.
  - US: MoCRA's fragrance allergen rule still pending (forum claim, unverified).
- **Verdict on lead 2: weak, leaning REJECT** (*inference* on the deciding point): a free-tool gap is real (two searches, none found) and the sum is error-prone, but every EU/GB cosmetic needs a signed safety assessment (CPSR) whose assessor checks the formula and is where makers are sent for this; the EU's 31 July 2026 date for new products has passed, GB hasn't adopted the expanded list; the input (each oil's allergen percentages) comes from suppliers' sheets typed in by hand or parsed from PDFs (the file-parsing cluster); and a "nothing to declare" result is false assurance on a legal label with a health consequence, the same risk class Labs rejected for food allergens (Batch 07) and for cosmetics formula checks (Batch 08). New evidence doesn't overturn Batch 08's reject.

### Lead 3: room reverberation time from a hand clap or balloon (seed)

- **Search 5** (teacher of the deaf, classroom acoustics, reverberation time, app, clap, BB93 or S12.60, without a consultant) (*source claim*, search summary and snippets):
  - The job exists as a screening step: Success for Kids with Hearing Loss (Karen Anderson) publishes a "Classroom Acoustical Measurement Exercise" for educational audiology and deaf-education courses (https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/Classroom-Acoustical-Measurement-Exercise.docx; assignment PDF https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/Classroom-Acoustic-Measurement-assignment.pdf): measure RT with a phone app and background noise with a sound level meter, compare with the recommendations, refer to an acoustical specialist if over. It warns "a clap is often not loud enough to generate good RT measures" and says read app reviews first.
  - Incumbent apps: Kröber "Nachhallzeit" / "Reverberation Time" and "Nachhallzeit Pro" (Android; trigger by a clap or a popped paper bag in a quiet room; average 3–5 readings; the standard version does "no frequency analysis or frequency weighting") (https://www.bluestacks.com/campaign/com.kroeber.reverberation_time_pro/fr). REW is the free desktop standard (known, not searched).
  - Rules: BB93 Tmf (mean of the 500, 1k, 2k Hz octave bands, unoccupied and unfurnished) ≤ 0.6 s for primary classrooms and ≤ 0.8 s secondary; the University of Edinburgh deaf-education course gives < 0.4 s for rooms for deaf pupils (https://www.ssc.education.ed.ac.uk/courses/deaf/dmar13i.html, not checked against BB93); ANSI/ASA S12.60 sets RT and background-noise limits (https://www.armstrong.com/en-us/articles/classroom-acoustics-ansi-standard.html). A broadband reading doesn't match Tmf's definition.
  - An ASHA paper may test phone accuracy (https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_AJA-19-0041); read next.

- **Reads 2 and 3 failed** (counted against the budget): the ASHA paper page returned HTTP 403, and PubMed's search page returned only a cookie notice. The paper's accuracy findings remain unread.
- **Verdict on lead 3: weak** (*inference*, on new evidence): the job is real but it's a screening step, and the screening tools already exist. Deaf-education and educational-audiology courses teach a phone-app RT check plus a sound level meter, with referral to an acoustician if the numbers look high (Success for Kids with Hearing Loss exercise, above); free and paid phone apps do the RT part (Kröber's, the standard version broadband only), and REW is the free desktop standard. A browser's wedge would be school-managed laptops and iPads where installing an app needs IT, and a BB93-style octave-band Tmf (500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz) printed with the readings. Against: the practitioners' own exercise warns a clap is often too quiet; the other half of the screening (background noise in dB(A)) can't be done credibly in a browser without calibrating the microphone; a phone figure can't show BB93 or S12.60 compliance, and a page would invite exactly that misuse. Batch 07's one-line reject was about uneven microphones; the decay rate is fairly robust to microphone response, so the better reasons are the existing apps and the screening-only status. Whether browsers deliver unprocessed audio (autoGainControl, noiseSuppression, echoCancellation off) on iOS Safari is a technical question the main session could test in WebKit, but it wouldn't change the need-side verdict.

### Lead 4 (own): collating a hand-press book against its collation formula (rare-book cataloguers, antiquarian booksellers)

- **The idea, need-first** (*inference*): before, a book on the desk and a formula from a bibliography or ESTC (e.g. π² A–2D⁸ 2E⁴(–2E4)), or gatherings counted by hand; after, a leaf-by-leaf list with expected signatures, a leaf and page count, a record of what's missing or cancelled, and the formula written in Bowers' conventions (23-letter alphabet without J, U, W; doubled alphabets 2A or Aa; π and χ for unsigned parts; ± for cancels).
- **Search 6** (collation formula tool, generator or calculator, signature statement, Bowers, missing leaves) (*source claim*, search summary and snippets):

  - No printed-book formula expander or checker surfaced. Practice is by hand from Bowers' *Principles* (https://whitneyannetrettien.com/whiki/index.php/Bowers_1949); the Massachusetts Historical Society's "Considering Collation: Decoding the Formula" posts teach reading one (https://www.masshist.org/beehiveblog/2013/11/considering-collation-decoding-the-formula-2); DCRM-L list threads ("Collation conundrum" 2013, "crazy signatures" 2015, "signature statement question" 2018, "Help with a signature statement" 2023: https://mailman.lib.byu.edu/pipermail/dcrm-l/2023-July/006893.html) are about notation choices, not arithmetic; the TEI Physical Bibliography group has worked examples (https://vault.tei-c.org/Workgroups/PB).
- **Verdict on lead 4: weak** (*inference*): a genuine method with no printed-book tool, and a static parser and checklist fit Labs well (offline in a reading room, phone at the book). But the audience is small and expert; expanding a formula is mental arithmetic for them, the hard parts (cancels, conjugacy, plates, inferred signatures) are judgment, the questions people actually post are about notation, and ESTC and bibliographies already give leaf counts to compare against. No evidence anyone wants this done for them.

### Lead 1, continued

- **Read 6** (University of Oregon, "Planning Shifts of Collections: spreadsheet", https://pages.uoregon.edu/chappell/shift/spreadsheet.html) (*verified*, the page itself): written instructions, not a calculator and no file; last substantive update 6 May 1997, with a 22 November 1999 note crediting a public librarian (Arlington Heights Memorial Library) with catching a formula error. Method: measuring sheets per range (start and end call numbers, sections, shelves, "hatch marks of empty space"); growth per shelf = (new shelves × 35 in + current growth space) ÷ total shelves; columns for growth space, inches available, inches of books, inches needed at a target fill (e.g. 20 in of books per 35 in shelf), sections needed = (inches needed ÷ 35) ÷ average shelves per single-faced section (6.5; 4.5 for oversize, 6 for tall journals, 10 for miniatures; an earlier version multiplied instead of dividing), running total; check by a second method; a different allowance for fast-growing areas, offset from slow ones (uniform for big, rushed shifts). Output: sections needed per range and a running total, feeding a "paper shift" where sections are coloured on maps to mark where each mini-shift begins and ends. ## Gate notes for the serious leads (A–H)

### Lead 1: collection shift planner (weak; the thread's best)

- **A. Audience:** staff who plan a stack shift: academic library stacks and access-services managers (Pittsburgh, Columbia, Penn, Emory, Boston College, Toronto Metropolitan, UCF above), branch and special librarians (VDOT), school librarians after weeding or new shelving, archivists moving a store; and the moving contractor who works from the map (*source claim*, the projects above; school and archive uses *inference*).
- **B. Immediate job:** before: the collection measured range by range (start and end call numbers, shelves, empty space; or item counts from the catalogue at about 11 books per foot) and the target shelving known (ranges, sections, shelves, shelf length). After: shelves or sections needed per class at the target fill, where each class or range starts on the new shelving (range, section, shelf, call number), inches to leave per shelf, a printable map for staff or movers, and "begins here" labels (*verified*, Oregon and Pittsburgh).
- **C. Why it matters:** rule-bound (75–80% fill, growth by class, call-number order, no class split between floors or ranges, buffers), labour-heavy (nine staff, 60–70 hours a week at Boston College; half a million books at TMU), error-prone (a formula slip in Oregon's own guide; Pittsburgh: the buffer "was critical"), and the map is the artifact the whole move runs on (*verified* and *source claim* as cited).
- **D. Current alternatives:** a spreadsheet built per project from published method (Oregon 1997; C&RL News 1990s; Fortriede's ALA templates); Alma Analytics plus Tableau plus a spreadsheet (Pittsburgh 2022); a "paper shift" colouring sections on maps; Harvard's CollectionShift (gone); the moving contractor (not searched). No free tool found in three searches.
- **E. Strongest incumbent:** the spreadsheet. Anyone who can write the running-total formula gets the numbers; the C&RL article itself says a spreadsheet is the right tool.
- **F. Labs wedge:** the method, the layout and the outputs in one place without building formulas: measure on a phone while walking the stacks (offline, where signal is often poor), get shelves needed and start points per shelf with the rules checked (fill, buffer, no split), a printable shift map and labels, and recalculation when a range is re-measured. Typed measurements, not a catalogue file (avoids the file-parsing cluster).
- **G. Technical fit:** good: arithmetic, localStorage, print. A catalogue-count route would mean reading exports (an avoided cluster) and should stay optional or out.
- **H. Against (searched):** for any one library it's an occasional project, not a weekly job; the arithmetic is a running total that spreadsheets do well; the hard parts are physical (measuring, inventory errors, moving); big projects use analytics or contractors, small shifts are often done by eye; all the evidence about the planning step is old (1990s, 1997, 2006), and no recent complaint about it surfaced.

A lead only if the main session finds that smaller libraries or archives shift often and struggle with the plan.

### Lead 2: fragrance allergen labelling (weak, leaning REJECT)

- **F.** Free, local (a formula is a trade secret, so local working is a real advantage), both lists side by side, summing across oils, a printable working sheet.

### Lead 3: reverberation time from a clap or balloon (weak)

- **A.** Teachers of the deaf and educational audiologists screening classrooms; also music teachers, choirs and small studios (home studios use REW).

- **C.** Rule-based and consequential for deaf pupils, but a screening step, not a measurement of record.
- **D.** Phone apps (Kröber's free and Pro), REW (free desktop), consultants; courses teach a phone-app check (Success for Kids with Hearing Loss).
