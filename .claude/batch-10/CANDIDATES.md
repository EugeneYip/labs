### Holiday pay: the deciding facts, checked here (`research/m-main-checks.md`)

- **The rule** (legislation.gov.uk, *verified*): the Working Time Regulations 1998 reg 16 applies the Employment Rights Act 1996 ss.221–224 to holiday pay with 52 weeks for 12, the calculation date the first day of the leave, weeks without remuneration skipped and earlier ones brought in, nothing before the 104 weeks (from 6 April 2020); for the 4 weeks of reg 13 and irregular-hours workers' leave under reg 15B, commission linked to the job, payments for status, and overtime "regularly paid" in the 52 weeks are added (from 1 January 2024); a week ends on Saturday unless the worker is paid weekly by weeks ending on another day.
- **A new record duty** (legislation.gov.uk, *verified*): reg 16B, inserted on 6 April 2026 by the Employment Rights Act 2025 s.35, requires records "adequate to show whether the employer has complied" with regs 13(1), 13A(1), 15B(2), 16(1) (pay at a week's pay), 14(2) and (6), and 15E(2), kept six years. So the working of each holiday-pay figure is now something an employer must be able to show.
- **The official method and examples:** GOV.UK's "Calculating holiday pay for workers without fixed hours or pay" (DBT, 23 July 2020, OGL; read here): count back from the last complete week before the leave, use only weeks with pay for work, leave out weeks of statutory payments such as SSP, leave out holiday weeks for workers with regular hours but include them for workers without, stop at 52 paid weeks or 104 weeks back; average weekly pay for workers without normal hours, an average hourly rate times usual hours for workers with normal hours whose pay varies; monthly pay spread over weeks by hours. Its worked tables (a monthly-paid worker, 52 weeks; an irregular-hours worker, 59 weeks with seven unpaid) give totals that don't match their own figures (*source claim* of the reader, to be recomputed from the tables in the build): the arithmetic is easy to get wrong even there.
- **The incumbents:** GOV.UK's own calculator does entitlement, not pay (*verified*); free "holiday pay calculators" take a total and a count of weeks you've already worked out (leavebalance.com, QX's, *verified*), so the week selection is left to you; Xero declined a 52-week average report on 27 November 2025 after 93 votes, with users "manually calculating everything in a spreadsheet" (*verified* by thread D); Moneysoft says it can't calculate holiday pay from the data it holds (*source claim*); paid HR and payroll systems sell it (Employment Hero, HiBob, Moorepay).

### Decision for #036 (2026-10-14)

- **BUILD → #036 Holiday Pay Average** (`holiday-pay-average`, homepage category `school`, for work): holiday pay for a worker whose pay or hours vary, by the 52-week rule, from week-by-week pay, with every week used or skipped shown and a record to keep.
  - **A. Audience:** UK small employers, their bookkeepers and payroll clerks, with staff on variable pay or hours (casual and zero-hours staff, regular overtime, commission, term-time and part-year workers) on payroll that doesn't do the average (Xero, HMRC's Basic PAYE Tools, a spreadsheet); and workers checking their own holiday pay (Citizens Advice's "check you've got the right amount" guide is for them).
  - **B. The job:** before, a worker has booked leave and the pay run is coming; the employer has the weekly or monthly pay history (payslips, a payroll report). After: the week's pay (and the day's or hour's rate) for that leave, from the right 52 weeks, with the weeks skipped and why, printed or saved for the six-year record.
  - **C. Why it matters:** every holiday, every pay run; the selection is error-prone (vendors' guides and the government's own tables slip); underpaying holiday is an unlawful deduction; the records duty is new and six years long; pay data is personal.
  - **D/E. Alternatives and the strongest incumbent:** a payroll export and a spreadsheet (Xero's own suggestion); paid payroll and HR systems that do it (Moneysoft's 52-week report, Employment Hero, HiBob); payroll bureaus and accountants. The strongest: changing to a payroll product that does it, or paying a bureau.
  - **F. Wedge:** no free tool selects the weeks: it takes week-by-week figures (typed, or pasted from a spreadsheet), applies the reference-period rules exactly (the end week, skipped weeks, SSP and statutory-leave weeks, holiday weeks by worker type, 104 weeks, fewer than 52), shows every week's fate, and prints the working; nothing uploaded, no account.
  - **G. Fit:** static and local; the rules are public and stable since January 2024; works offline.
  - **H. Against, and why BUILD survives:** (1) liability: which payments count (is overtime "regular"?) is the employer's legal judgment, so the page takes that as the employer's input, says so, and decides nothing it can't; (2) the input sits near the export-reading pattern of #034 and #035, so it reads only a pasted or typed table, not payroll exports; (3) rolled-up pay at 12.07% sidesteps the average for some irregular-hours workers since 2024, but not for regular-hours workers with variable pay, nor for employers who haven't adopted it; (4) employers whose payroll already does it don't need it. The evidence of the friction (Xero's declined request and its comments in 2026, the new duty, the slips in published examples) is current and specific.
  - **Diversity:** a new domain for Labs (employment and payroll) and a new interaction (labelling a pay history week by week and seeing a statutory rule select from it).
