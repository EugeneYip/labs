### Writing and lists (thread finished 2026-10-13)

- **Citation cross-check: REJECT.** Saturated with free tools since 2025. CiteMe's In-Text Citation Checker is free, needs no sign-up, says it runs in the browser, and handles author-date and numbered styles (*source claim*, citeme.app/tools/in-text-citation-checker). RefRunner says the same (*source claim*). Recite is paid beyond 2 uploads a day, uploads files, and needs a Google login (*verified*). Paul Beverley's free CitationAlyse macro works in Word. Labs would be about the tenth.
- **Consistency check for long documents: REJECT.** The job ends inside Word, where it's already covered: PerfectIt (offline on Windows; on Mac and iPad its cloud version uploads the document, *verified*), Paul Beverley's free macros (local, PC and Mac), and Intelligent Editing's free Google Docs add-on (*verified*). A separate browser report adds a step and fixes nothing.
- **Compare two lists of names: open (decided below with the other results).** Flookup's free Fuzzy Matching Tool runs in the browser with no sign-up but gives pair similarity scores, not "missing from each side" lists, and has no name-specific handling (*verified* from its page; not tried). molbiotools List Compare is exact matching only (*verified*). Excel's fuzzy merge in Power Query exists in Microsoft 365 and is technical (*verified*). The thread's search allowance ran out before forum evidence of how often non-technical people hit name-format mismatches.

- **Small-club year-end accounts: HOLD.** Unresolved: can most small-club treasurers download a whole year as CSV from their bank (CAF Bank, Unity Trust, Co-op, high-street community accounts)? If not, the tool falls back to typing every line, which the free cashbooks from parent bodies and the Charity Commission's CC16a template already handle (*verified*: CC16a is free, no account, last updated 2017). Timely: for years ending on or after 30 September 2026, independent examination is needed only above £40k (*verified* by the thread, gov.uk). Yearly use makes lost browser storage a real risk.
- **Home blood pressure averages: REJECT.** Trivial arithmetic in a setting run by clinicians' own protocols and forms; protocols differ (NICE, Blood Pressure UK, BHF, AHA); any "high/normal" label drifts toward a regulated medical device (MHRA guidance, *verified* by the thread). Free record cards exist (Blood Pressure UK, BHF).
- **Raffle tickets: REJECT.** At-event raffles need nothing printed (Gambling Commission, *verified* by the thread) and use cloakroom ticket books; advance sales have Avery's free numbering or cheap perforated printing; plain paper means hours of cutting.

### Exam clock: the decisive rule, checked here

JCQ's *Instructions for conducting examinations*, effective 1 September 2026 (jcq.org.uk/knowledge-hub/intructions-for-conducting-examinations/, read 2026-10-13, *verified*): §11.7 "A reliable clock (analogue and/or digital) must be visible to each candidate in the examination room when seated… The clock must show the actual time. Countdown and 'count up' clocks are not permissible." §11.8: a board must show "the actual starting and finishing times and date of each examination." §6.1: "Candidates must always be allowed the full amount of time for the examination." §12.6: an invigilator may have a mobile phone only to summon help. So a timer-style display can't be the exam-room clock, and the board's finish times are what candidates rely on.

### Documents and privacy (thread finished 2026-10-13)

- **Redact before sending: REJECT.** Already built, free and on the device: Jasper Bernaers' PDF Redactor (search and "redact all matches", hand-drawn boxes, pages rebuilt as images, then the saved file re-checked; no account; *verified* by the thread), BentoPDF (local, AGPL), PDFGadget (local, image output). And the riskiest recipients refuse the output: the High Court held on 19 May 2026 that DWP "is entitled to require unredacted statements" (Webb v SSWP [2026] EWHC 1581 (Admin), *verified* by the thread); US rental screening through Snappt marks edited or re-rendered PDFs "Denied: Edited Docs" (*verified* by the thread). Building it could hurt users.
- **Watermark an ID copy: REJECT.** Solved where the practice is common: FiligraneFacile (French government, free, no account; files processed on its servers and deleted), DossierFacile watermarks rental files, the Dutch KopieID app; and a watermark protects little. Would be a feature of Photo Scrub, not an experiment.
- **Move-in condition report: REJECT.** Phone photos plus an email are the evidence deposit schemes want (Shelter, *verified* by the thread), and burden-of-proof rules do the rest; the move-out comparison needs a year of storage, which Safari's 7-day cap on unvisited sites undermines.
- **Anonymize interview transcripts: open, a strong survivor (checked below).** The free options are a highlight-only Word macro (UK Data Service: it "does not anonymise or make changes", *verified* by the thread), QualiAnon (free local Java desktop app, .docx only, 16 GB RAM recommended), Python libraries, and paid apps (Textwash Pro $330/yr, MAXQDA turns text into "XXX"). Researchers avoid uploading interviews (the free local transcribers noScribe and aTrain exist for that reason, and neither anonymizes). GDPR counts data as pseudonymized only with the key "kept separately" (Art. 4(5)). Unfinished: a search for browser-based transcript pseudonymizers. Risk: suggestions can falsely reassure about indirect identifiers (job titles, events).

### Music, hiring, theater (thread finished 2026-10-13)

- **Part-writing checker: open, a narrow survivor.** Free coverage is partial: MuseScore's "Check Harmony Rules" plugin (free, local, MuseScore 4.4+) checks parallel, contrary, and exposed 5ths and 8ves and leaps, but not crossing, spacing, range, doubling, or leading-tone and seventh resolution (*verified* by the thread); Musition has the full rule set at $39 a year per student through a school code (*verified*); Counterpointer is $39.99, Mac only. Also overlaps Off Book's parser; the availability arithmetic is a spreadsheet job. Doesn't block the batch.

## Decisions after the gate (2026-10-13)

- **BUILD → #026 Exam Timekeeper** (`exam-timekeeper`). Checked here before numbering:
  - **The rule** (*verified*, JCQ ICE effective 1 September 2026, §11.7–11.8): the exam-room clock must show the actual time; countdown and count-up clocks aren't permitted; a board shows the actual start and finish times.
  - **The pain** (*source claim*, The Exams Office, November 2024): "a large number of candidates" have supervised rest breaks, there's "confusion in a significant number of centres about how these breaks should be managed", and breaks have increased because JCQ requires them to be trialled in mocks and timed tests before 25% extra time is applied for. A single break should be no more than 30 minutes and isn't normally needed in the first 10 minutes. Universities and professional bodies run the same "stop the clock" breaks with paper slips on the desk showing the revised finish (LSE, IFoA, Teesside; *source claim*).
  - **The alternatives** (*verified* today): The Exams Office's Exam Room Clock Tool needs a centre membership and a login, and pauses only the whole room; ExamClock needs an account, and individual rest-break pausing is in Pro at $89 a year; Mega Seating Plan's free exam clock is a countdown, which JCQ exam rooms can't use; a £5 Tes HTML timer adds fixed 5-minute breaks for up to 10 students (no reviews); keesL/ExamClock (GitHub) shows the time with a closing countdown and no provisions. No free, no-account tool found shows the actual time and the board and pauses individual candidates.
  - **Rounding:** no JCQ rule found; awarding bodies' worked examples differ (AQA uses seconds, Pearson rounds). The tool counts to the second and only ever rounds a shown finish time up.
  - **Strongest case against:** paper slips work and exams officers are cautious; a wrong finish time is maladministration; a centre may not let staff use an unapproved site; The Exams Office could add individual breaks. None is fatal: the arithmetic is small and testable, nothing leaves the laptop, and the record prints.
  - **Name:** no product called "Exam Timekeeper" found; "ExamClock" and "Exam Room Clock Tool" are other products' names, so they're avoided.
- **Next survivors, in order:** anonymize interview transcripts (after its one search: browser-based transcript pseudonymizers); part-writing checker (narrow). Each goes through its own final check after #026 ships.
- **HOLD (don't block the batch):** compare two lists of names (does Flookup's free tool already give "missing from each side"?); tournament day schedule (does a free, no-account tool schedule pitches with rest gaps offline?); volunteer rota (does a free, no-sign-up tool make a fair rota for 20+ people?); cross-country scoring (do enough meets score by hand?); small-club year-end accounts (can treasurers get a year of bank CSV?); scene breakdown (is script parsing accurate enough?).

## Decisions for #027 (2026-10-13)

- **BUILD → #027 Transcript Pseudonyms** (`transcript-pseudonyms`). Checked here before numbering:
  - **No free, no-install tool:** FORS's 2026 guide to anonymisation tools (forscenter.ch, read 2026-10-13, *verified*) lists for qualitative data the UK Data Service toolkit (manual, limited on Mac), QualiAnon (semi-automatic, desktop, "still under development"), ATLAS.ti/NVivo/MAXQDA (manual, proprietary), Textwash (desktop, English and Dutch), and Microsoft Presidio (for programmers), and advises against uploading non-anonymised data to cloud services. Maynooth's IQDA tool is a Java program fed a hand-made list of names (*source claim*). The closest new tool, an Obsidian plugin for conversation-analysis transcripts (local name detection, key kept out of exports), is desktop-only with 231 downloads (*verified*). A Chrome extension pseudonymizes by regex patterns only (*source claim*). Transcription services tag names at most (Sonix), and the APIs that redact (Amazon Transcribe, AssemblyAI) need uploading and a developer, and give "[PERSON_NAME]" rather than consistent pseudonyms (*source claim*).
  - **The need:** guidance from the UK Data Service, Erasmus University Rotterdam, Maynooth, and others asks for consistent pseudonyms across transcripts and warns that search-and-replace makes unintended changes and misses variations; researchers keep writing their own scripts (a 2025 criminology Python script, the Obsidian plugin). GDPR counts data as pseudonymized only with the key kept separately (Art. 4(5)).
  - **The wedge:** no install (managed university laptops often allow no Java or Python), nothing uploaded or stored, consistent pseudonyms across a project through a key file the researcher keeps, and a guarantee general AI tools can't give: nothing changes in the text except what the researcher chose, with a count for each.
  - **Strongest case against:** many researchers manage with find-and-replace, and universities offer approved AI (Copilot with data protection); a tool's suggestions could reassure falsely about indirect identifiers. Answered by never claiming anonymity, showing what's left (capitalized words not replaced), and a deterministic replacement whose every change is listed. Names that are also ordinary words (Will, May, Grace) are the main correctness risk.
  - **Name:** "Transcript Pseudonyms" avoids "anonymize", which a key-based replacement isn't.

## Decisions for #028 (2026-10-13)

- **BUILD → #028 Hidden Data Check** (`hidden-data-check`, category `files`). Checked here before numbering:
  - **A. Who:** people who send or publish spreadsheets made from personal data. First, FOI and information-governance staff in councils, NHS trusts, police forces, universities, and schools (Tes, 3 September 2025, writes to school DPOs about it); then anyone sending a workbook outside their organisation.
  - **B. The job:** before a spreadsheet goes out, find everything in it that isn't on screen, see what it holds, and remove it. Today that's unhiding by hand, Excel's Document Inspector, or converting to CSV. After: open the file in a page that lists every hidden place the ICO names, shows what each holds, says how to remove it in Excel, and sends the file nowhere.
  - **C. Stakes and recurrence** (*verified* unless marked): ICO guidance of 31 July 2025 (ico.org.uk, "How do we avoid an accidental breach when personal information is hidden in spreadsheets?") lists hidden rows, columns, and worksheets; personal information "in remote rows or columns"; active filters; pivot tables, charts, slicers, timelines, and cube formulas that keep a cache; links to external sources that store a cache; and embedded data models. It says tools "may be able to detect some, but not all", that Document Inspector "cannot identify when personal information is hidden in remote rows and columns", and recommends converting to CSV where appropriate. In its first three months, mySociety's analyser screened 3,064 spreadsheets sent through WhatDoTheyKnow and stopped 21 confirmed and 53 likely breaches, about 2% (mySociety, 23 May 2024, *source claim*). Its reports to authorities and the ICO "has not improved behaviour" (*source claim*, same statement). Hammersmith and Fulham was reprimanded in May 2025 for an FOI spreadsheet with 35 hidden worksheets, ten holding the personal data of 6,528 people, 2,342 of them children, published for almost two years (ICO and Local Government Lawyer, *source claim*). PSNI was fined £750,000 in 2024 for a hidden tab. Islington (£70,000) and Kensington and Chelsea (£120,000) were fined over pivot-table caches.
  - **D. Alternatives:**
    - **Document Inspector** (Windows desktop Excel). It reports pivot caches, external links, embedded files, filters, and hidden names, but can't remove them (Microsoft's support page, *verified*). It misses objects covered by other objects and shapes in hidden columns (*verified*). It misses remote rows and columns (ICO, *verified*), and shows no contents. Not in Excel for Mac (SumProduct, Microsoft Tech Community, *source claim*), and no web version was found.
    - **Free in-browser tools,** each covering a slice (all *verified* from their own pages):
      - ExcelErrorFinder: a formula auditor that also flags hidden and very hidden sheets and hidden rows and columns; its page names no pivot caches, external caches, data models, comments, properties, remote cells, or text coloured to vanish.
      - ExcelToolkit Pro's File Inspector: macros and external connections.
      - MetaClean and metadatafinder: document properties.
      - Kanaries and ExcelTool.io: viewers.
    - **The rest:**
      - mySociety's analyser: only for files sent through WhatDoTheyKnow, after they're sent.
      - Python scripts for programmers: pivot-table-to-csv, ExcelExpose, xlsx-toolkit.
      - Windows freeware: Hidden Data Detector (2016) and Hidden Text Detector (Zenodo, 2025).
      - Enterprise metadata cleaners.
    - **No free tool found** checks the ICO's list without an upload.
  - **E. Wedge:** uploading a file to check it would itself disclose it, so only a page that reads it locally can help. It works on Macs, Chromebooks, and locked-down laptops. It covers the whole ICO list, finds what Document Inspector can't (remote cells, values hidden by a `;;;` format or by text coloured like its background, pivot caches whose source sheet was deleted), shows what each place holds (a cache's records, a hidden sheet's first rows), and says how to remove each in Excel.
  - **F. Fits Labs:** static; a local zip and XML reader like #027's; no services; offline; works on a phone with a file from Files or Drive.
  - **G. Strongest case against:**
    1. The fix is procedural (paste values into a new workbook, or send CSV), and authorities already have Document Inspector. Breaches come from not checking, which no tool forces.
    2. A false "nothing found" could reassure someone into a breach; no checker can prove a file is safe.
    3. IT rules may bar unapproved sites even when nothing is uploaded.
    4. OOXML is large, and .xls, .xlsb, .ods, and encrypted files can't be read here.
    5. ExcelErrorFinder already catches the most common case, hidden sheets.
  - **Why BUILD survives:**
    1. The checks keep failing despite the guidance (the 2024 rate of about 2%, the 2025 reprimand). Seeing the contents ("a pivot cache holds 943 rows: Name, Address…") is what makes the risk concrete, and many senders don't have Windows Excel.
    2. Never say "safe": list what was checked and what can't be, and keep the ICO's CSV advice in view.
    3. As with #027: nothing is sent, and it works offline.
    4. .xlsx and .xlsm only (the format of the breaches); other formats are refused with a way forward.
    5. Pivot caches, external caches, and remote cells are what ExcelErrorFinder doesn't name, and they caused the Islington and Kensington and Chelsea fines.
  - **Name:** no product called "Hidden Data Check" found; "Hidden Data Detector" (2016 Windows freeware) is the nearest.

## Discovery for #029 (2026-10-13)

The diversity rule shapes this round: #027 and #028 are both mainly files and privacy, so a third there needs to be clearly the strongest. That sets aside the parked Word-comments and small-number-suppression leads for now. The strongest lead outside that domain is allocation by ranked choices, checked here with nine searches.

- **Allocating people to activities by ranked choices: BUILD → #029** (below).
  - **A. Who:** whoever turns ranked choices into places with limits. First, school staff running enrichment, electives, clubs, and activities weeks (a teacher with about 180 students and 12–15 activities a week, whose two staff "sift through" paper slips by hand: MrExcel forum, *source claim*); then university project and module allocation, conference and staff-training workshops, camps and youth groups.
  - **B. The job:** before, choices collected on a form or slips, then allocated by hand, by a spreadsheet's rounds, or by first come, first served. After: paste or open the form's responses, set each activity's places, and get everyone placed as high on their list as the places allow, with each activity's list, who got which choice, and anyone who couldn't be placed and why.
  - **C. Recurrence and stakes:** weekly or termly enrichment, yearly activities weeks and project allocation, each conference. Fairness is visible: children and parents compare who got their first choice, and a hand allocation can't show it was fair.
  - **D. What people use today** (*verified* unless marked):
    - **Scheduler by Alice Keeler** (Google Workspace add-on: 70K+ users, "free of charge, with paid features", updated July 2025). It needs a Google Form with one grid question, and the organizer grants it Docs and Sheets access, "send email as you", and running in their absence. It assigns greedily: it "cycles through the activity list looking for participants 1st choice", then the next rank. Not for Microsoft Forms, which many UK schools use.
    - **Moodle's Fair Allocation (Ratingallocate)** and Allocation Form: they maximize overall satisfaction with capacities, but only inside a Moodle course.
    - **Institutional systems:** Southampton's Choices, Bath's Fair Allocation, Harvard's Automated Allocation (logins, their own students).
    - **Paid platforms:** Clipboard's preferential activity selection (a school platform), StatsDirect (statistics software), event platforms such as Happenee; Cvent users ask how to collect ranked session choices and assign on the back end (*source claim*). PowerSchool can't take ranked elective choices (its forum, *source claim*).
    - **Code and DIY:** GroupMatcher (Go), students-projects-assigner (Python), assign-projects (ASP, with minimum sizes), an R Shiny app used for 400 delegates at the GSS Conference 2018, a 2024 Scott Logic series (its assigner "improved fairness over hand-calculated assignments by 30%"), Criteo Labs, a StuKon 2023 student project, a "Data Science for Schools" Python article (2024), and an Excel Solver attempt that found no feasible solution (*source claim* for these write-ups).
    - **Not the same job:** Clever Groups forms teams from who wants to work with whom (account, free trial of 20 generations); TeamSort and RankedChoices are polls; GroupMixer and SORT make random groups.
    - **No free tool found** that allocates by ranked choice with places per activity, without an account or an install.
  - **E. Wedge:** any form's responses (Microsoft Forms' ranking question, Google Forms' grid or one question per choice, or typed in), no account and no Google permissions, nothing uploaded (pupils' names stay on the device), an allocation that's best for the whole group by a stated rule rather than greedy rounds, minimum sizes (an activity too few chose is dropped and its people re-placed), a lottery for ties that can be replayed, and each activity's list to print or download.
  - **F. Fits Labs:** static; the allocation is a min-cost flow, fast in a browser for thousands of people; files in and out (CSV); works offline.
  - **G. Strongest case against** (searched):
    1. Many schools book clubs and activities first come, first served (school booklets; ParentPay- and Arbor-style booking) or run a draw, so not all of them collect ranked choices.
    2. Google schools have a free incumbent with 70K+ users.
    3. The page only does the middle of the job: choices still come from a form, and results go out by hand.
    4. "Best for the group" can be argued: giving one person their fifth choice so three get their first may look unfair.
    5. Activities weeks and conferences often have several sessions, each person in a different activity in each: a harder problem.
  - **Why BUILD survives:**
    1. The evidence of pain is broad and recurring: hand sorting at weekly scale, people writing their own code across schools, universities, and conferences, and platform users asking for exactly this.
    2. Against the incumbent: Microsoft schools have nothing; greedy rounds leave people lower on their lists than an allocation for the whole group would; and no account or Google permissions are needed.
    3. The rule is stated and shown: as many first choices as possible, then as many second choices, and so on (rank-maximal), with every person's result and the totals by rank visible, and the lottery's tie-breaks replayable from a number.
    4. Scope cut to keep it small and correct: one place per person (several distinct places only if it stays simple); sessions over several time slots left out.
