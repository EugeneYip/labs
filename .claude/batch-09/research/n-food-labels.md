  - Additives: by "the name of their functional class, such as antioxidant or preservative", then the specific name or E number.
  - "May contain": "not a legal requirement of food law", recommended, and it "should only be provided if a real risk of allergen cross-contact has been identified".

  - Placement: on the package or on a label attached to it; the consumer "should not need to open the packaging".
  - Responsibility and change: "You need to use the information provided from suppliers to produce accurate labels"; have "processes in place to update this information should there be a change in suppliers, or if ingredients change".

## The job (before → after)

- **Before** (*verified* in the FSA/FSS evaluation unless marked): for each PPDS item, the seller works out the ingredients list in descending order of weight, copies each compound ingredient's sub-ingredients from the supplier's label, emphasises every allergen every time, names additives by class, adds "may contain" where they judge a risk (72% do), and prints. 64% print in-house, often with bought label software and printers (one owner spent "50 hours" programming; another "nearly £2,000" on equipment); 25% buy printed labels; 8% handwrite (15% of cafés). Without software the route is a Word, Avery or Canva template typed and bolded by hand, or MenuCal's per-recipe output (*source claim*). The chore recurs: 63% check suppliers' ingredients at every delivery or often, 78% of those who update labels do so every time a supplier changes, and a small caterer names "the time it takes to change this and print off an extra label" as the burden.
- **After** (*inference*): an ingredient library (each bought ingredient with its supplier list and the allergens the seller marks) and products built from it by weight; the page produces the ordered list with compounds nested in brackets and the marked allergens emphasised wherever they occur, warns when the text won't fit the chosen label at a 1.2 mm x-height, flags every product that uses an ingredient the seller has just changed, and prints on A4 sheets.

- **Free**: Food Standards Scotland's **MenuCal** (free; account; recipes, a "store cupboard" of ingredients, allergens, calories; PPDS label output, a Word document per recipe according to a snippet; maintained); Avery UK's editable Natasha's Law template and Avery Design & Print Online (free, no account, per Batch 08; no ordering or emphasis logic); Word and Canva templates; Brother P-touch Editor (free desktop software for Brother printers); MenuSano's free allergen checker (keyword detection, no label); foodlabelmaker.com's "Get a Free Label" (a taster; *inference*).
- **Paid**: Planglow LabelLogic Live, £120 a year or £13 a month (2022 price); Labl.it from £28 a month (NCASS partner, 14-day trial); foodlabelmaker.com about $49 a month (US listings); Nutritics, Kafoodle, Gen-Label (Bunzl, fed by Erudus supplier data) at prices not found; tills and scales that print allergen labels (a deli in the evaluation); wholesalers' trials (Thomas Ridley).
- **Strongest incumbent**: **MenuCal** (menucal.fss.scot), run by the Scottish regulator: free, does the recipe-to-PPDS-label job with an ingredient library, and keeps the data in an account rather than in a browser. Its gaps (an account; Scotland-branded, with eligibility elsewhere unconfirmed; the label still laid out and printed by the baker) are real but small. For anyone paying, about £10 a month buys a supported tool with templates and printers.

## Labs-fit problems

- **The settled safety class.** Batch 07 rejected allergen labels as a safety risk and Batch 08 filed them under legal or safety interpretation. Narrowing the page to "format what the baker marks" doesn't take it out of that class: the output is still the business's legal allergen declaration, and the page would be doing the step the evaluation's deli describes as the software's job ("puts allergens in bold and sorts the formatting"). A formatting fault (a marked allergen in a nested sub-ingredient left plain, a line cut to fit, water ordered wrongly) is a recall-grade error: five of the year's 69 FSA allergy alerts (four with full titles seen) were for allergens present but "not emphasised" (*verified*, page read 3).
- **Detection must stay out, and its absence is a trap.** Keyword matching is unsafe ("butter" and "whey" don't say milk, spelt and couscous are wheat, *inference*), so the page can only trust the seller's marks; but a page that lays out the label invites the belief that it checked them (*inference*).
- **The value is the ingredient library, kept in a browser.** Losing it when browser data is cleared means re-entering every supplier list; a café or kitchen with several staff and devices would need sharing, which Labs can't do. MenuCal's account exists for exactly this (*inference*).
- **Printing.** Label sheets are Batch 08's settled fragile family (Safari page size only from 18.2, WebKit bug 164201, unknown printer margins); many in-house printers bought label software and hardware (evaluation), and roll label printers driven by their own software are a poor target for browser printing (*inference*). A label printed too small is non-compliant, and small type is consumers' top complaint.
- **Rule depth.** Annex VII's water and volatile-ingredient ordering, compound-ingredient exemptions, fortified flour's added nutrients, additive classes, QUID for meat products, and FSS's separate guidance in Scotland all have to be right. The rules look stable (*verified*: the FSA guide still carries its 17 June 2021 publication date and no later update), which helps upkeep, but 55% of councils want the full-ingredients-list rule replaced by allergens only, a possible future change (*verified*, evaluation).
- **Phones.** Building a recipe library and printing label sheets are both desktop jobs (*inference*).

## Strongest reason not to build

A missed or unemphasised allergen on a PPDS label can kill: the law exists because Natasha Ednan-Laperouse died in 2016 after eating a PPDS baguette (*verified*, evaluation). A Labs page would author that legal declaration with no checking it can safely do. It would rest on the seller's marking just as a Word template does, while adding failure points of its own (nesting, fitting, ordering) and an impression that something was checked. Meanwhile the regulator already gives a free tool, 81% of businesses say compliance is easy, and 68% want no more support (*verified*, evaluation). The non-compliance most councils have met is no label at all (89% of councils) and missing or incomplete ingredient lists (84%, 82%), failures a formatting page doesn't reach, since the hard part (getting and tracking suppliers' lists) stays with the seller.

## Thread verdict

**Reject.** The need is real and the regulator documents it (among the 11% who find compliance hard, the time to introduce and update labels is the top reason, and businesses with 1 to 5 staff comply least: 69%). But the regulator's free MenuCal already does the job, sellers' own forum voices never surfaced in three tries, and narrowing the page to formatting leaves it in the allergen-safety class Labs settled in Batch 07, with Batch 08's printing fragility on top.
